## Why this matters

Here is a question that looks too easy to get wrong. You have 240 measurements of a quantity
$y$ that depends on a setting $x$ through $y = |x|$, with a little noise, and for some reason
no measurements were taken for $-0.5 < x < 0.5$. Which setting gives $y = 0.75$? The approach
from [Lecture 9](../l09/notes.md) is to fit a model of $x$ as a function of $y$ and ask it.
A least-squares line through these data answers $x \approx 0.00$. The true answers are
$x = -0.75$ and $x = +0.75$. The fitted value is their average, and it is not an answer at
all: no measurement anywhere near it gives $y = 0.75$.

A more flexible model does not rescue this. A Gaussian process or a neural network trained
by minimizing squared error on data with two branches also predicts the mean of the
branches, because the mean is what minimizes squared error. Regression learns one number per input, $E[x \mid y]$, and an inverse question with two
answers does not have one number.

Engineering is full of such questions. The Peng-Robinson equation of state for propane at
300 K and 10 bar is a cubic in the compressibility factor $Z$ with three real roots: a liquid
root at $Z = 0.0348$, an unstable middle root at $0.1280$, and a vapor root at $0.8147$.
Newton's method returns whichever root its starting guess happens to lead to. In the right
panel of the figure below, starts spread uniformly over $[0, 1]$ reach the liquid root 20% of
the time, the unstable root 37% and the vapor root 43%. Nothing in the answer tells you the
other two exist, and the basins are not even contiguous: starts between about 0.45 and 0.57
jump all the way down to the liquid root.

```{figure} figures/regression-failure.png
:alt: Left, data on two straight branches forming a V with a gap in the middle, a steep orange regression line through the gap, and the horizontal line y = 0.75 crossing the data at x = -0.75 and +0.75 while the regression gives x near 0. Right, the Peng-Robinson cubic residual against Z with three roots marked, and a bar beneath colored by which root Newton's method reaches from each starting guess.
:width: 100%

Two inverse questions with more than one answer. Left: a least-squares fit of $x$ on $y$
returns the average of the two answers. Right: Newton's method on the Peng-Robinson cubic
finds one root, chosen by the starting guess. Both panels are generated by
`figures/make_figures.py`.
```

This session is about models that answer such questions properly. A **generative model**
learns the whole distribution of the data, inputs and outputs together, instead of one
function from inputs to an output. Once you have the distribution you can fix any variables
you know and sample the rest, which returns every answer consistent with what you fixed.
The same idea is underneath every product called "generative AI": an image generator, a
molecule generator and a chatbot all learn a distribution from data and sample from it,
usually conditioned on something you supplied. Seeing the idea in two dimensions, where
every sample can be checked, is the clearest way to understand what those large systems are
doing and where they fail.

## What a generative model learns

```{index} generative model, discriminative model, joint distribution, conditional distribution
```

Treat every row of a data set as a draw from an unknown probability distribution. For the
$|x|$ data a row is the vector $(x, y)$, and the distribution $p(x, y)$ is a density over the
plane that is high along the two branches of the V and zero elsewhere. A generative model is
a model of that distribution, learned from the rows.

:::{admonition} Definition: generative model
:class: tip
A **generative model** learns the probability distribution of the data well enough to draw
new samples from it, and sometimes to evaluate the probability density at a point.
:::

Those two abilities, sampling and evaluating the density, are separate. Some models do both;
some, including the flow-matching models later in this session, only sample. Sampling is the
one that matters for the inverse questions above.

The rows do not have to be measurements of a function. An image is a grid of pixel values,
and flattening the grid gives a long vector: a 512 × 512 color image is a vector of 786,432
numbers. A collection of photographs is then a cloud of points in a space of that dimension,
with strong correlations between neighboring coordinates, and generating a new photograph
means drawing a new point from that cloud and reshaping it into a grid. Alves and Kitchin
make this observation the starting point of their paper on generative optimization
([ChemRxiv, 2026](https://chemrxiv.org/doi/full/10.26434/chemrxiv-2025-hk886/v3)), the anchor
reading for this session. A row of inputs, outputs and derivatives from an engineering model
is the same kind of vector, only much shorter. Whatever works for images works in principle
for $(Z, r(Z))$, and the short vector has the advantage that every sample can be checked.

### Joint, marginal and conditional

Three distributions come out of one data set. The **joint distribution** $p(x, y)$ describes
the inputs and outputs together. A **marginal**, such as $p(y)$, ignores some variables: it is
the histogram of $y$ alone. A **conditional**, such as $p(x \mid y = 0.75)$, fixes some
variables and describes the rest: it is the slice of the joint along the line $y = 0.75$,
renormalized. For the $|x|$ data that slice has two peaks, at $\pm 0.75$, and sampling it
returns both answers in proportion to how much data supports each.

Conditioning is the operation that turns a generative model into a solver. Fix the outputs
and sample the inputs, and you have solved an inverse problem. Fix the inputs and sample the
outputs, and you have a forward prediction with its uncertainty. Fix some of each and you have
a question that regression cannot even express, such as "given this feed temperature and this
product purity, what reflux ratio and steam rate are consistent with the plant's history?"

### Discriminative and generative models

The models of Lectures 8 to 14 are **discriminative**: they learn $p(y \mid x)$, or often
only its mean $E[y \mid x]$, and they answer in one direction. A generative model of the
joint answers in any direction, at the price of modeling more. The table sets the two side by
side.

| | Discriminative (regression, classification) | Generative |
|---|---|---|
| What is learned | $E[y \mid x]$ or $p(y \mid x)$ | $p(x, y)$, or a way to sample it |
| Which questions | inputs to outputs | any variables given any others |
| What comes back | a number, or one distribution over $y$ | samples, every mode included |
| What it must model | how $y$ depends on $x$ | that, and how $x$ is distributed |

### Three ways to make a distribution learnable

Writing down a distribution over many variables is hard, and there are three common ways to
make it tractable. They organize the rest of this session.

```{figure} figures/families.png
:alt: Three panels. Left, samples from a four-component Gaussian mixture forming the V shape. Middle, gray Gaussian noise and orange points that have flowed onto the V. Right, a bar chart of illustrative next-word probabilities after the phrase "the reactor outlet temperature is".
:width: 100%

Three ways to make $p(x)$ learnable. The bar heights in the right panel are illustrative,
not the output of a model.
```

The first is to choose a flexible family of densities and fit its parameters, then condition
it with algebra. A mixture of Gaussians is the standard example, and it is the first approach
developed below. The second is to give up on writing the density and instead learn a
transformation that carries an easy distribution, usually a standard normal, onto the data.
Normalizing flows, diffusion models and flow matching all work this way; this is the second
approach below. The third is to factor the joint with the chain rule of probability,

$$p(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = p(x_1)\,p(x_2 \mid x_1)\,p(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2) \cdots = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_{<i}),$$

which is exact, and to learn each conditional "what comes next, given everything so far."
Sampling then draws $x_1$, then $x_2$ given $x_1$, and so on. This is called an
**autoregressive** model. It is the family that language models belong to, where each $x_i$
is a word or a piece of a word; this session names it and leaves it there.

## Learning the joint distribution: Gaussian mixture models

```{index} Gaussian mixture model, expectation maximization, Schur complement
```

```{index} see: GMM; Gaussian mixture model
```

The first approach writes the joint density down explicitly. One Gaussian is too simple for
a V-shaped cloud, but a weighted sum of several Gaussians can follow any smooth shape if it
has enough of them.

:::{admonition} Definition: Gaussian mixture model
:class: tip
A **Gaussian mixture model** writes a density as a weighted sum of $K$ Gaussians,
$p(\mathbf{z}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)$,
with weights $\pi_k$ that sum to one.
:::

Start with one Gaussian over $(x, y)$. Its mean vector says where the cloud is, and its
covariance matrix says how the cloud is stretched and tilted. The off-diagonal covariance
$\Sigma_{xy}$ is the linear relationship between $x$ and $y$, so a single Gaussian is linear
regression viewed as a joint distribution. A mixture of $K$ Gaussians covers a curved
relationship with $K$ locally linear pieces. For the $|x|$ data two components suffice, one
tilted ellipse along each branch.

### Fitting by expectation maximization

The parameters are the weights, means and covariances of every component. They are fitted by
maximizing the likelihood of the data, and the standard algorithm is **expectation
maximization** (EM), introduced in its general form by [Dempster, Laird and Rubin
(1977)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1977.tb01600.x). EM alternates two steps. The
E-step computes, for every data point, a *responsibility* for each component: the probability
that the point came from that component under the current parameters. The M-step recomputes
each component's weight, mean and covariance as averages over the data, weighted by those
responsibilities. Each pass cannot decrease the likelihood. If you have seen k-means
clustering, EM is k-means with soft assignments and with clusters that have shapes, which is
why Gaussian mixtures are also a clustering method.

EM converges to a local maximum, and which one depends on where it starts. scikit-learn's
`GaussianMixture` runs it from several starts (`n_init`) and keeps the best. The interactive
figure below runs EM in your browser; press Play to watch the ellipses find the branches, and
try $K = 1$ to see one Gaussian give up on the V entirely.

### Choosing the number of components

```{index} Bayesian information criterion
```

```{index} see: BIC; Bayesian information criterion
```

More components always raise the likelihood on the training data, so the likelihood alone
cannot choose $K$. The **Bayesian information criterion** (BIC) adds a penalty proportional to
the number of parameters, and the $K$ with the lowest BIC is a reasonable default. For the
$|x|$ data, BIC falls from 543 at $K = 1$ to $-697$ at $K = 2$ and rises again to $-673$ at
$K = 4$: two components are best, matching the two branches. Held-out log-likelihood, computed
on data the mixture did not see, is the other honest check, and it is the same idea as the
held-out test sets of Lectures 8 to 10.

### Conditioning a mixture

A Gaussian has a property that makes it unusually convenient: fix some of its coordinates,
and the distribution of the rest is again Gaussian, with a mean and covariance given in closed
form. Partition the variables into the ones you fix ($k$, for known) and the ones you want
($u$), and partition the mean and covariance to match. Given the known values $\mathbf{v}$,

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_{u \mid k} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_u + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{uk}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{kk}^{-1}(\mathbf{v} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k),
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{u \mid k} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{uu} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{uk}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{kk}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ku}.$$

The second expression is called the **Schur complement**. The first says the conditional mean
moves from the unconditional mean along the regression line of $u$ on $k$; the second says the
conditional spread is the original spread minus what the known values explain.

For a mixture, condition each component $j$ this way and then reweight the components by how
well each one explains the known values,
$\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{v}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,j}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{kk,j})$, renormalized.
Here $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,j}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{kk,j}$ are component $j$'s mean and
covariance restricted to the known variables. The result is again a Gaussian mixture, so sampling it is exact and immediate.
At $y = 0.75$ the two branch components each explain the value equally well, each conditional
is a narrow Gaussian centered near its branch's answer, and the samples split between
$x \approx -0.75$ and $x \approx +0.75$. Move the $y^*$ slider in the figure above to watch the
lower panel; the orange line is the regression's single answer.

The surprise here is that the split between inputs and outputs is chosen after training. The
same fitted mixture answers $p(x \mid y)$, $p(y \mid x)$, and any mixed question, because
conditioning is a function call with the known columns as an argument. Nothing about which
variables are inputs was decided when the model was fitted. The notebook's `condition`
function, about twenty lines of NumPy, is the whole method. The [gmr
library](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.03054) (Fabisch, 2021) packages the same
computation as Gaussian mixture regression.

### Root finding, optimization and parameter estimation

Alves and Kitchin turn this into a uniform recipe for problems that are normally solved by
specialized algorithms. In each case you generate or collect rows containing the variables
you will search over and a quantity you want to fix, fit the joint, and condition.

```{index} pair: case study; Peng-Robinson roots
```

**Root finding.** Sample $Z$ uniformly on $[0.01, 0.99]$, evaluate the Peng-Robinson residual
$r(Z)$ at each sample, fit a mixture to the 2,000 rows $(Z, r)$, and condition on $r = 0$.
All three roots come out of one set of samples. The paper's Table 1 reports 0.035 ± 0.001,
0.128 ± 0.010 and 0.814 ± 0.002, against true roots of 0.0348, 0.1280 and 0.8147. The course's
own fit, twelve components on standardized $(Z, r)$, puts the clusters at 0.0440, 0.1273 and
0.8143. The vapor and unstable roots are within 1%, and the liquid root is 26% high. The liquid
root sits in a narrow region of $Z$ where the cubic changes quickly, so the result there is
sensitive to fitting choices such as $K$, the scaling of the columns and the random seed. One
fix that works here is to fit the same mixture to $(\log Z, r)$, which spreads the small values
of $Z$ out. That gives 0.0346 for the liquid root, at the cost of a slightly worse unstable
root, 0.1257 (1.8% low). Choosing the representation is part of the modeling, as it was for the
features of [Lecture 7](../l07/notes.md).

The samples are starting points, and the honest last step is
to polish the center of each cluster with a few Newton iterations against the real cubic. In
the notebook that turns 0.0440, 0.1273 and 0.8143 into 0.0348, 0.1280 and 0.8147, identical
to `numpy.roots`. The generative model's contribution is to find every basin at once; the
solver's contribution is precision.

**Unconstrained optimization.** At an interior optimum the derivative is zero, so condition
on it. For consecutive reactions $A \to B \to C$ in a batch reactor with $k_1 = 0.5$ and
$k_2 = 0.2\ \mathrm{min}^{-1}$, the intermediate $C_B(\tau)$ peaks at
$\tau^* = \ln(k_1/k_2)/(k_1 - k_2) = 3.05$ min. Sample $\tau$, compute $C_B$ and
$dC_B/d\tau$ (by automatic differentiation, as in [Lecture 11](../l11/notes.md)), fit the joint
distribution, and condition on $dC_B/d\tau = 0$. The paper fits
$p(\tau, C_B, dC_B/d\tau)$; the notebook drops $C_B$ and fits $p(\tau, dC_B/d\tau)$, which
is enough for this question. The paper's GMM gives a mean of
2.89 min and its flow-matching model 3.04 min. In the notebook, the GMM's samples have a median
of 3.13 min, with the middle 90% between 2.93 and 3.33 min. Conditioning on a zero derivative
finds every stationary point. A function with a maximum and a minimum would return both, and a
second condition or the second-derivative test tells them apart.

**Constrained optimization.** With constraints the objective's gradient is not zero at the
optimum, but the gradient of the Lagrangian is, so the paper adds the Lagrange multipliers as
columns and conditions on all the partial derivatives of the Lagrangian being zero. One example is a
gasoline blend of reformate, isomerate and alkylate that must meet an octane specification.
There the GMM's samples average $x = (0.284, 0.607, 0.109)$, the same as the `scipy` SLSQP
solution, with standard deviations below 0.01 in each fraction. Inequalities are handled with
a log-barrier term instead.

**Parameter estimation.** Simulate a plug-flow reactor for many pairs of rate constants
$(k_1, k_2)$, record the exit flows $(F_A, F_B)$, fit the joint of all four, and condition on
the flows you observed. The answer is a distribution over rate constants, the set of
parameters consistent with the observation, rather than one fitted pair. Its width is the
kind of uncertainty [Lecture 14](../l14/notes.md) asked you to interpret with care: it mixes
noise in the data with the surrogate's own error.

### Where Gaussian mixtures stop

A full covariance matrix in $D$ dimensions has $D(D+1)/2$ entries, and every component has
one, so a mixture's parameter count grows with $D^2$ and EM's cost with $D^3$. Gaussian
mixtures are a good tool for tens of variables. They are hopeless for an image, where $D$ is
in the hundreds of thousands, and they struggle with relationships that are sharp or strongly
curved, which need many components. That limit is what the second approach removes.

## Transforming a simple distribution: flows, diffusion and flow matching

```{index} inverse transform sampling, change of variables, normalizing flow, diffusion model
```

The second approach never writes the data's density down. Instead it learns a function that
transforms samples of a simple distribution, usually a standard normal, into samples of the
data. You already know one generator that works this way.

### Inverse transform sampling

To draw from any one-dimensional distribution, draw $u$ uniformly on $[0, 1]$ and return
$x = F^{-1}(u)$, where $F$ is the target's cumulative distribution function. This is
**inverse transform sampling**, and it is a generative model with a known transformation.
Where the target density is high, $F$ is steep, so a given range of $u$ maps onto a narrow range
of $x$, and samples pile up there.

<div class="cw" data-widget="inverse-cdf"></div>

That piling up is the **change of variables** rule. If $x = g(z)$ with $g$ invertible, the
densities are related by
$p_X(x) = p_Z(z)\,\left|\det \partial z / \partial x\right|$: a map that compresses volume
raises the density, and one that stretches volume lowers it. Any learned transformation has
to keep track of that factor if it is going to report a density. In one dimension the
transformation is the inverse CDF, which you can tabulate. The difficulty is learning one in
many dimensions.

### Normalizing flows

A **normalizing flow** builds the transformation from a stack of invertible layers, each
designed so its Jacobian determinant is cheap to compute, and fits it by maximum likelihood
using the change-of-variables formula
([Rezende and Mohamed, 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05770); the [review by Papamakarios et
al., 2021](https://jmlr.org/papers/v22/19-1028.html), is the standard reference). Flows give an
exact density and exact sampling. The price is the architecture: every layer must be
invertible with a tractable determinant, which rules out most of the network designs from
[Lecture 12](../l12/notes.md). Invertible networks have been used directly for engineering
inverse problems ([Ardizzone et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04730)).

### Continuous flows

```{index} continuous normalizing flow, probability flow ODE
```

Instead of stacking layers, let the transformation be the solution of an ordinary
differential equation. Start a point at $x(0)$ drawn from the normal distribution and move it
with a velocity field,

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = v_\theta(x, t), \qquad 0 \le t \le 1,$$

and call $x(1)$ the sample. This is the [neural ODE of Lecture 13](../l13/notes.md), used to
move a whole distribution of starting points instead of one trajectory
([Chen et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07366), who called these *continuous
normalizing flows*). Any smooth velocity field gives an invertible map, so the architecture
restriction disappears. Training by maximum likelihood, however, means solving the ODE inside
every training step, which is slow.

### Diffusion models

**Diffusion models** take a different route to the same kind of map. A fixed forward process
adds a little Gaussian noise to the data at each of many steps until nothing but noise is
left. A network is trained to undo one small step at a time. Generating a sample means
starting from noise and applying the learned reverse steps
([Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585);
[Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239)). [Song et al.
(2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456) showed that the process can be written as a
stochastic differential equation, and that it has a deterministic twin, the *probability flow
ODE*, which is a continuous flow of the kind above. Diffusion is the method behind most image
generators of the last several years, and it is the approach the weather, protein and
materials models in the survey below use.

### Flow matching

```{index} flow matching, rectified flow
```

Flow matching keeps the continuous flow and replaces its expensive training with
regression ([Lipman et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02747), who describe it as a
simulation-free way to train continuous normalizing flows;
[Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03003), who call the same idea
*rectified flow*). It is the simplest of these methods to understand and to implement, so
it gets the full treatment here.

:::{admonition} Definition: flow matching
:class: tip
**Flow matching** trains a velocity field $v_\theta(x, t)$ by regression onto the velocities of
straight lines from noise samples to data samples, then generates data by integrating
$dx/dt = v_\theta$ from noise at $t = 0$ to $t = 1$.
:::

One training example is built in four steps. Draw a data sample $x_1$, a noise sample
$x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, and a time $t$ uniformly on $[0, 1]$. Form the point on the
straight line between them,

$$x_t = (1 - t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1 .$$

The velocity of that line is $x_1 - x_0$. Train the network to predict it, with the
mean-squared-error loss
$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\,\lVert v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \rVert^2$.
That is the whole training procedure: a regression, with the same optimizer and loss as
[Lecture 9](../l09/notes.md) and [Lecture 11](../l11/notes.md), and no ODE is solved during
training. To generate, draw $x_0$ from the normal distribution and integrate $dx/dt = v_\theta$
from $t = 0$ to 1. The paper uses 100 Euler steps, $x \leftarrow x + v_\theta(x, t)\,\Delta t$.
Its version of the path adds a small noise floor $\sigma$ so the target stays finite near
$t = 1$, which changes nothing essential.

To make the model conditional, give the network the condition as an extra input,
$v_\theta(x_t, c, t)$, and train on pairs $(x_1, c)$ from the data. For the $|x|$ problem, $x_1$
is $x$ and $c$ is $y$. At sampling time you choose $c$, and the flow carries noise to
$p(x \mid c)$. In the figure below, set $y^*$ and press Play: sixty noise samples flow to the two
branches. The gray arrows are the velocity field.

<div class="cw" data-widget="flow-condition"></div>

The field in that figure is not a trained network. For a finite data set $\{x_{1,i}\}$, the
velocity field that minimizes the flow-matching loss has a closed form. At each point $(x, t)$
it is the average of the straight-line velocities of the training points whose lines could
pass through $x$ at time $t$, weighted by how likely that is:

$$v^*(x, t) = \sum_i w_i(x, t)\,\frac{x_{1,i} - (1 - \sigma)\,x}{1 - (1 - \sigma)\,t},
\qquad w_i \propto \mathcal{N}\!\left(x;\ t\,x_{1,i},\ (1 - (1 - \sigma)\,t)^2\right).$$

The figure computes this field exactly in your browser, with the paper's noise floor
($\sigma = 0.02$). It conditions on $y^*$ by multiplying each weight by a Gaussian kernel of
width 0.04 in $y$, so strictly it is the minimizer for those kernel-weighted data. It shows what
a network is trying to learn. The field is exact for the data, so its samples land on training
points. A network cannot represent the field that exactly, and
its smoothness is what lets it produce samples between the training points. That is also the
first sign of a failure mode discussed under the limitations below: a model that fits its
training data too well reproduces it.

### Why the paths curve

Every training example is a straight line, yet the trajectories in the figure curve, and
samples split between the branches only partway through. The reason is that the network sees
many lines through each point and learns their average velocity. Near $t = 0$, a point at
$x = 0.1$ lies on lines heading to both branches, so its average velocity is small; only later,
when the lines through a point all head the same way, does the field commit. The figure below
makes this concrete in two dimensions. On the left, noise samples move along straight lines to
randomly paired data points, which is what training examples look like, and the lines cross.
On the right, the same noise samples follow the averaged field. An ODE assigns one velocity to
each point at each time, so no two particles are ever at the same place at the same time,
although a path may pass where another path was earlier. At every $t$ the two clouds have the
same distribution, and the readout compares their means and spreads. Rectified flow
([Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03003)) and minibatch optimal transport
([Tong et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00482)) are ways of choosing the pairs so the
paths are straighter and fewer integration steps are needed.

### The same problems, by flow matching

On the $|x|$ data, a conditional flow-matching model sends samples conditioned on $y = 0.75$
to both branches, with means near $-0.74$ and $+0.76$. The model is a three-layer network of 64
units, trained for 4,000 minibatches in about ten seconds on a CPU. On the Peng-Robinson roots
it finds the vapor root, but it blurs the liquid and unstable roots into one broad band between
0 and 0.15, and changing to $\log Z$ does not separate them. Between those two roots $|r|$ never
exceeds 0.0016, which is 3.6% of the standard deviation of $r$ across the training data, so the
condition $r = 0$ barely distinguishes them. The Gaussian mixture conditions in closed form and
resolves them; the network has to learn the conditioning, and it learns it smoothly. The
paper's Figure 4 shows flow matching resolving all three roots, with its own architecture
(sigmoid activations) and longer training (500 epochs). The comparison here is one data point
about sensitivity to those choices.

## Where generative models fit in machine learning

```{index} self-supervised learning
```

Every model in this session is a statistical model fitted to data by optimization, the same
kind of object as the models of earlier lectures. Placing generative models among those methods
is the fastest way to predict what they can and cannot do.

### Supervised, unsupervised and generative

Machine learning is usually divided by what the data contains. **Supervised learning**
fits a map from inputs to labeled outputs, which is what Lectures 8 to 12 did. **Unsupervised
learning** finds structure in unlabeled data: clusters, low-dimensional representations,
densities. Generative modeling began on the unsupervised side, as density estimation, and
the Gaussian mixture was a clustering method long before anyone used it as a solver. A
conditional generative model, however, is trained on pairs $(x, c)$ exactly as a supervised
model is; it just learns the whole conditional distribution instead of its mean. Models
trained on very large unlabeled corpora blur the line further, because the "label" for each
piece of the data is another piece of the same data, which is called *self-supervised*
learning.

The more useful distinction for an engineer is between kinds of question. Use a
discriminative model when every input has one answer and you only need that answer. Use a
generative model when an answer can have several values, when you need to ask in more than one
direction, or when you need samples, for instance to propagate uncertainty or to propose
candidates.

Generative models also sit next to Bayesian inference. A Bayesian treatment of an inverse
problem combines a prior on the parameters with a likelihood from a forward model and samples
the posterior, usually by Markov chain Monte Carlo, which requires evaluating the forward model
many times. A generative model learned from simulated or measured pairs reaches the same kind
of answer, a distribution over parameters given an observation, without a likelihood at
inference time. This is the idea behind **simulation-based inference**
([Cranmer, Brehmer and Louppe, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01429)). The cost moves to
training, and the result is only as good as the data and the fit.

### Still data-driven

A generative model is a surrogate model of the data, and everything [Lecture 9](../l09/notes.md)
and [Lecture 14](../l14/notes.md) said about surrogates applies to it without exception.

It needs data, and it needs more than a regression for the same job. A regression models how
$y$ depends on $x$; a generative model of the joint also models how $x$ is distributed, which is
extra work that has to be paid for in samples. Density estimation gets harder quickly as the
dimension grows, which is why the image and molecule models in the survey below are trained on
millions of examples.

It is only valid where the data are. Conditioning on a value outside the training data does
not raise an error. The model returns samples anyway, built from whatever its building blocks
do far from data. The notebook asks both models about $y = 0.3$, inside the gap, where the
true answers are $\pm 0.30$, and both answer without hesitation. The Gaussian mixture says
$-0.31$ and $+0.31$, close to right only because its straight ellipses extend the straight
branches; it would be wrong for a curved function. The flow-matching model says $-0.41$ and
$+0.33$. This is
the extrapolation failure of earlier lectures, and it is harder to see here, because in many
dimensions it is not obvious which conditions are inside the data.

It inherits the data's flaws. Bias in what was measured, gaps in the operating range and
mislabeled rows all end up in the samples. A model of a plant's historian data learns the
plant as it was operated, including the operating policy, so it cannot tell you about regions
the operators never visited. That can be useful, because the historian already encodes the
constraints the plant runs under, which is the argument the paper makes for its evaporator
example. It is also a limit, because the optimum might be somewhere the plant has never been.

It answers questions about the surrogate. Every sample is a solution of the learned
distribution, which may or may not be a solution of the system. When an exact model exists, it should check the samples, as the Newton
polishing above did. When only data exist, the samples are hypotheses to test, and the
generative model is the cheapest way to propose them.

And it is still a model with hyperparameters: the number of components, the network size, the
training length, the number of integration steps, and the representation of the variables, as
the $\log Z$ result showed. Each needs the held-out evaluation and seed-to-seed comparison the
earlier lectures required of any model.

### Pros and cons against the alternatives

The table compares three ways of answering an inverse or optimization question: a
conventional solver applied to a model, a discriminative surrogate, and a generative model.

| | Solver on a model (Newton, SLSQP, IPOPT) | Discriminative surrogate | Generative model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Needs a model of the system | yes | no, needs data | no, needs data |
| Answers in which direction | the one it was posed in | inputs to outputs | any direction |
| Several solutions | one per starting point | averages them | all, as samples |
| Accuracy | to solver tolerance | surrogate error | surrogate error, plus sampling |
| Cost of a new value of the condition | a new solve | one evaluation | one batch of samples |
| Certificate of optimality | KKT conditions checked | none | none |
| Scales to high dimension | yes, with structure | yes | flows and diffusion yes, GMMs no |

Alves and Kitchin are explicit that generative optimization does not replace mathematical
programming. Its strengths are finding every solution at once, working from data when no
model exists, and answering a new condition without solving again. Its weaknesses are the
weaknesses of the surrogate underneath it.

### Choosing between the two approaches

```{index} pair: failure mode; conditioning variables fixed at training time
```

Within generative models, the two approaches of this session trade off in a predictable way.

| | Gaussian mixture | Conditional flow matching |
|---|---|---|
| What is learned | a density: weights, means, covariances | a velocity field (a neural network) |
| Training | EM, seconds | gradient descent, seconds here, hours or more at scale |
| Sampling | an exact draw | an ODE solve, about 100 network calls per sample |
| Conditioning | any variables, chosen after training | only the variables fed to the network in training |
| Density at a point | exact | not directly available |
| Dimension | tens of variables | up to millions |

The conditioning row is the one most often missed. A Gaussian mixture can be asked
$p(x \mid y)$ today and $p(y \mid x)$ tomorrow. A conditional flow-matching model trained to
generate $x$ given $c$ can only answer that question; asking a different one means training a
different model.

## Generative models in science and engineering

```{index} generative adversarial network, variational autoencoder, latent space
```

```{index} see: GAN; generative adversarial network
```

The same two ideas, learn a distribution and sample it conditionally, run through the large
systems that made generative AI a household phrase and through a growing body of scientific
work. For each domain below, ask the same two questions: what is generated, conditioned on
what, and how were the samples checked?

### Images

Two model families came before diffusion. **Generative adversarial networks**
([Goodfellow et al., 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)) train a generator against a
discriminator that tries to tell generated samples from real ones. **Variational
autoencoders** ([Kingma and Welling, 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114)) learn an encoder
into a low-dimensional **latent space** and a decoder back out, and generate by decoding random
latent points. Both are transformation models in the sense of this session: they map a simple
distribution onto the data.

The current generation of image models is built on diffusion and flow matching. [Rombach et
al. (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752) ran diffusion in the latent space of an
autoencoder rather than on pixels, which made high-resolution generation affordable. The
[Stable Diffusion](https://github.com/CompVis/stable-diffusion) models are latent
text-to-image diffusion models of this kind, conditioned on a text prompt. [Esser et al.
(2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03206) scaled rectified flow, the straight-path flow
matching above, to text-to-image models, and Stability AI's
[Stable Diffusion 3](https://stability.ai/news/stable-diffusion-3-research-paper) uses that
formulation. The two-dimensional flow in this session's figures and a text-to-image model share
a training objective and differ in scale, architecture and conditioning signal.

### Molecules

Molecules are discrete graphs, which makes them awkward to generate. [Gómez-Bombarelli et al.
(2018)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5833007/) trained a variational autoencoder on
SMILES strings, so that molecules became points in a continuous latent space that could be
searched by gradient-based optimization for desired properties. The paper is candid about the
central problem: without care the latent space has "dead areas" that decode to invalid
SMILES strings, which is why every molecule a generator proposes has to be checked for
validity before anyone believes it. Later work generates 3-D structures directly, with
diffusion models that respect rotations and translations
([Hoogeboom et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17003)), and uses diffusion to generate
the pose of a drug molecule docked in a protein
([DiffDock, Corso et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01776)).

### Proteins

```{index} pair: case study; RFdiffusion
```

[RFdiffusion (Watson et al., 2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10468394/)
generates protein backbones by diffusion, conditioned on design requirements such as a binding
target or a symmetry. Its check was the laboratory. The authors characterized the structures
and functions of hundreds of designed proteins experimentally. The cryo-electron microscopy
structure of a designed binder in complex with influenza haemagglutinin was "nearly identical
to the design model." [AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al.,
2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11168924/) predicts the joint structure of
proteins, nucleic acids and small molecules with what its authors call a "substantially
updated diffusion-based architecture": structure prediction itself has become conditional
generation, with the sequence as the condition.

### Materials

```{index} pair: case study; MatterGen
```

The Crystal Diffusion Variational Autoencoder ([Xie et al.,
2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06197)) generates periodic crystal structures. [MatterGen
(Zeni et al., 2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11922738/) is a diffusion model
for inorganic materials that can be fine-tuned to generate structures with a target property.
The authors generated 8,192 candidates for each of four target bulk moduli, selected four for
synthesis after expert inspection, and succeeded with one: TaCr₂O₆, in a compositionally
disordered version of the ordered structure the model predicted. The target was 200 GPa, and DFT
on the ordered structure gave 222 GPa. The measurement was indirect: nanoindentation measured a
Young's modulus, and the bulk modulus was estimated from it with a DFT-computed Poisson ratio.
The paper reports the estimate as "up to 169 GPa after four measurements (158 ± 11 GPa)."
The abstract's "within 20% of our target" holds at 169 GPa, the upper end of that range. At the
central value, 158 GPa, the estimate is 21% below the target. The synthesis is a real result,
and the headline figure depends on which end of the reported range you read. [Park, Li and Walsh
(2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matt.2024.05.017) ask the broader question directly in the
title of their perspective, "Has generative artificial intelligence solved inverse materials
design?"

### Weather

```{index} pair: case study; GenCast
```

[GenCast (Price et al., 2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11666454/) is a
conditional diffusion model that generates the next global weather state, conditioned on the
current and previous states, and rolls forward to build a forecast. Because each sample is a different plausible future, an
ensemble of samples is a probabilistic forecast, which is generation used as uncertainty
quantification in the sense of [Lecture 14](../l14/notes.md). It produces a 15-day forecast at
0.25° resolution in about 8 minutes on one Cloud TPU v5 device. It had greater skill than the
European Centre's operational ensemble, ENS, on 97.2% of the 1,320 combinations of variable,
lead time and level evaluated. Its check is the standard one in forecasting: it was trained on
reanalysis from 1979 to 2018 and scored against what the weather actually did in 2019.

### Process engineering and optimization

The anchor paper applies the methods of this session to root finding, optimization,
parameter estimation and the mapping of a desired output region back to the design inputs
that produce it. Its capstone is an evaporator flowsheet with twelve process equations. The training data
were optimal and suboptimal solutions over a grid of constraint settings, with noise added to
mimic plant measurements. The models were conditioned on the active constraints and on all
twelve equation residuals being zero. The best GMM sample reached an optimality gap of 1.61%
against the `scipy` SLSQP solution, and the best flow-matching sample 5.83%. Both best samples
were near-feasible, with a largest constraint violation just under the paper's threshold of
0.1. [Kitchin
(2025)](https://doi.org/10.1039/D5DD00137D) works through a single inverse problem with
generative models in more detail, and the [review by Anstine and Isayev
(2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10141264/) surveys generative models across the
chemical sciences.

:::{admonition} What a practitioner should take from this
:class: tip
- In every successful example above, the generator proposes and something else verifies: a
  laboratory, a held-out season of weather, DFT, or the physical model. Plan the verification
  before you train the generator.
- Read the measurement behind a headline number, as with MatterGen's 169 GPa against a central
  value of 158 GPa.
- Validity checks are part of the method. A generated molecule, crystal or operating point is
  not an answer until it has passed them.
:::

## Limitations and trade-offs

```{index} pair: failure mode; memorization
```

```{index} pair: failure mode; model collapse
```

```{index} pair: failure mode; extrapolating outside the training data
```

The limitations follow from what a generative model is: a fitted surrogate of a data
distribution, sampled at random.

**The samples answer questions about the surrogate.** A sample is consistent with the learned
distribution, which can differ from the system. The course fit's liquid root at 0.0440 instead
of 0.0348 is the small version of this; a generated molecule that cannot be synthesized is the large one.
Check samples against the real model wherever one exists.

**Conditioning outside the data returns confident output.** There is no warning when a
condition is outside the training data. In two dimensions you can see the gap; in two hundred
you usually cannot, and in high dimensions it is not even obvious how to define "inside."

**Sharp structure is missed.** Features that the data do not resolve, such as two roots
separated by a residual that never exceeds 0.0016, are blurred or lost, in fitting and in
sampling. More data where the structure is, or a better representation such as $\log Z$, helps
more than a bigger model.

**Conditioning can be fixed at training time.** A conditional flow-matching model answers only
the question it was trained for. Gaussian mixtures are flexible here and limited in dimension.

**Validity is not built in.** Generated molecules can violate valence, generated crystals can
be unstable, and generated operating points can violate constraints the data did not make
explicit. The paper's constrained examples succeed because the constraints were added as
conditions.

**Models memorize.** A generative model fitted too closely to its training set reproduces
training examples, as the exact flow field in this session's figure does by construction.
[Carlini et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13188) generated 175 million images from
Stable Diffusion, 500 for each of the 350,000 most-duplicated training captions. They found 94
that matched training images closely enough to count as extracted under their definition, and
13 more near-copies on manual inspection. For engineering data that means a model trained on
proprietary or personal records can leak them.

**Training on generated data degrades a model.** [Shumailov et al.
(2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11269175/) showed that models trained
recursively on the output of earlier models lose the tails of the original distribution, and
that this "model collapse" occurs in language models, variational autoencoders and Gaussian
mixture models alike. Generated samples are not new data, and a pipeline that feeds them back
in as training data is making the model narrower.

**Evaluation has no single number.** A regression has a test error. A generative model's
samples can be individually plausible and collectively wrong, missing a mode or overweighting
one. For engineering problems the best evaluation is usually the physical model: compute the
residual, the constraint violation or the property for every sample, as the root-finding and
evaporator examples did.

## Summary

A generative model learns the distribution of the data instead of one function of it, and
conditioning that distribution answers questions in any direction, returning every answer
consistent with what you fixed. Gaussian mixtures write the joint density down, fit it by
expectation maximization and condition it exactly with the Schur complement; they are fast,
flexible in what they condition on, and limited to tens of variables. Flow matching learns a
velocity field that carries noise to data, trains by plain regression on straight-line
velocities, and samples by integrating an ODE; it scales to images and molecules but answers
only the conditional question it was trained for. The Gaussian mixture found all three
Peng-Robinson roots from one set of samples, where Newton's method from one guess finds one,
and its samples needed Newton polishing against the real cubic to be exact. Flow matching found
the vapor root and blurred the two close ones. Underneath the names, generative AI is
data-driven modeling. It needs data that cover the questions you will ask, it is valid only
where those data are, and it answers for the surrogate. Its samples are proposals for something
else to verify.
