## Why this matters

Every model you build eventually produces one number that leaves the room with you. It goes
in a slide, a report, a pull request description: "RMSE of 3.4 megawatts," "92% of faults
detected." That number is not a measurement of your model. It is a **prediction about the
future**, specifically about how the model will behave on data it has not seen, generated by
a process you have only partially observed. Everything in this session exists to make that
prediction honest, because the machinery that produces it is unusually good at producing
numbers that are too good.

The failure is not that people lie about their results. It is that the ordinary,
well-intentioned workflow leaks optimism at half a dozen points, each of them individually
defensible. You try a few models and report the best one, so the number you report is the
maximum of several noisy draws rather than a typical one. You tune a hyperparameter by
looking at the validation score, so the validation score has stopped being independent of the
model. You split your rows at random, and the rows turn out to have come in batches from the
same specimen, the same wind-tunnel run, the same engine, so your test set is full of near
copies of your training set. None of these produces an error message. All of them produce a
number that is smaller than the truth, and a smaller number looks like better work.

The engineering consequence is that the model is deployed against a promise it cannot keep. A
plant-output surrogate that reported ±3 MW in the notebook and delivers ±8 MW in production
does not merely disappoint; it invalidates whatever downstream decision was sized against the
±3. If the surrogate feeds a dispatch optimisation, the optimisation was solved against a
model of the model's error that was wrong by a factor of three. This is why the discipline in
this session is not statistical fussiness. Reporting a number you cannot defend is an
engineering defect in the same category as an unverified tolerance.

The clearest public illustration of the whole failure mode is Google Flu Trends, which is
covered below. It was built by competent people, was published in *Nature*, held out a
validation season correctly, and reported a 0.97 correlation on data it had genuinely not
seen. It then spent two years predicting flu prevalence that was consistently far too high,
and was eventually beaten by the least sophisticated possible baseline: last week's numbers
from the CDC. This session is about the difference between those two states, and about the
handful of habits that separate them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this session you should be able to:

- Build and beat baselines; know when a "sophisticated" model is not actually earning its

- Choose the correct cross-validation scheme for the data's structure.

## The supervised workflow, and the number you only compute once

The supervised workflow has a canonical shape, and the reason to name its stages explicitly
is that the stages are what keeps the accounting straight. You start with **problem framing**:
what quantity is being predicted, from what inputs, at what time, and to support what
decision. Then a **baseline**, the simplest thing that could possibly work, whose score is the
bar every later model has to clear. Then a **model family**, then **validation**, which is
where you compare candidates, then **selection**, where you pick one, then a single
**held-out test** evaluation, and finally **error analysis**, where you look at what the
chosen model actually gets wrong rather than at the average of what it gets wrong.

The stage that gets abused is validation, because validation and testing feel like the same
activity. They are not. Validation data is a *tool you consume* during model development: you
look at it, you make decisions based on it, and every decision you make transfers a little
information from that data into your model. Test data is a *measurement instrument*, and it
works exactly once. The moment you look at a test score and change anything (a
hyperparameter, a feature, the model family, even the decision to keep iterating rather than
stop) that test set has been converted into validation data, and it will no longer give you
an unbiased answer. There is no way to un-look.

This is worth stating as a rule because it is so easy to violate accidentally: **the test set
is touched once, at the end, and the number that comes out is the number you report.** If you
do not like it, you do not get to try again, and the correct response is to go back to the
validation data, not to run a second test.

:::{admonition} Common pitfall
:class: warning

The most common version of this in student work is not dishonest at all. It is reporting the
*best validation score* as though it were the model's expected performance. It is not: it is
the minimum over everything you tried, and the minimum of a set of noisy numbers is
systematically lower than any of them deserves. The size of that optimism is measured later in
these notes, and it depends almost entirely on how much data you have.
:::

Framing deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the stage where an engineering problem
becomes a machine learning problem and where most of the consequential choices are made
silently. This session's dataset is the [UCI Combined Cycle Power Plant
set](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/294/combined+cycle+power+plant): 9,568 hourly
records from a plant operating at full load between 2006 and 2011, with four ambient
measurements (temperature, exhaust vacuum, atmospheric pressure, relative humidity) and one
target, net hourly electrical output in megawatts. Framing this as supervised regression
embeds several assumptions worth surfacing. It assumes the plant configuration is fixed
(true here by construction: the data was collected at full load only). It assumes ambient
conditions cause output rather than the reverse, which is physically right for a gas turbine,
where inlet air density sets mass flow. And it assumes each hour is a separate question,
which is where things get interesting, and which the cross-validation section returns to.

This is the shape of an enormous number of engineering problems: **a cheap-to-measure input
predicting an expensive-to-measure performance quantity**. Pump curves, engine maps,
compressor performance, process yield, and the whole business of surrogate modelling have
this form. When you can measure ambient conditions with a $200 sensor and the thing you
actually care about requires a calibrated power meter or a week of CFD, a model that maps one
to the other is worth building, and knowing how much you can trust it is the entire question.

## Baselines that have to be beaten

A baseline is not a formality and it is not a courtesy to the reader. It is the *unit* in
which every later result is denominated. Without one, an RMSE of 3.4 MW is an uninterpretable
number. With one, it has a position on a ladder that runs from "predicting a constant" down to
"the best anyone in the study managed," and you can see at a glance which end it is near.

The baselines worth building in engineering contexts are short and specific:

- **Predict the mean.** For a regression target, this is the floor. Its RMSE is, by
  construction, the standard deviation of the target, and any model that cannot beat it is
  actively worse than a constant.
- **Persistence, or predict the last value.** For anything time-ordered, this is the baseline
  that matters, and it is far stronger than people expect. Tomorrow's temperature is very
  nearly today's temperature. A model that cannot beat persistence is not a model, it is an
  expensive way of describing the recent past.
- **A linear or ridge model.** Cheap, interpretable, and much better than its reputation on
  physical data, because a great many physical relationships are locally linear in the range
  you sampled.
- **A single decision tree.** Different failure mode from linear, so it tells you something
  different: if a shallow tree does well, your relationship is more like a lookup table than
  a smooth function.
- **A physics or domain-heuristic baseline.** An ideal-gas calculation, a published empirical
  correlation, an operator's rule of thumb. This one is the most valuable and the most often
  skipped, because it is the only baseline that encodes what is already known about the
  system.

Run these on the power-plant data and the result is a useful shock.

On the right, magnified to the 3 to 6 MW range, the ladder from a single-variable linear model at 5.43 down to a random forest at 3.36 is visible.

Predicting the mean costs **17.07 MW**, which is the target's own standard deviation to three
figures, as it must be. A linear regression **on ambient temperature alone** costs 5.43 MW.
That single number is the most important one in this session: one thermodynamic variable and
a straight line close **85% of the entire gap** between having no model and having the best
model in the study. Adding the other three ambient channels takes you to 4.56 MW, or 91%. The
random forest, at 3.36 MW, closes the remaining 9%.

The physical reason is not mysterious, and it is worth stating because it is what makes the
number a lesson rather than a curiosity. A gas turbine breathes air. Colder air is denser, so
the compressor ingests more mass per unit volume, so the machine makes more power. Ambient
temperature is not one predictor among four; it is *the* predictor, and everything else is a
correction. Any engineer on the plant could have told you that before a model was fitted, and
a baseline is how that knowledge gets into the study.

:::{admonition} What a practitioner should take from this
:class: tip

Report your results as a ladder, not as a single number. "The random forest achieves 3.36 MW
RMSE" is a fact about nothing. "Predicting the mean costs 17.07, a straight line in ambient
temperature costs 5.43, and the random forest costs 3.36" tells the reader what the model is
worth, what it would cost to replace with something maintainable, and whether the last
increment justifies a dependency on scikit-learn in a control room.

And keep the fold-to-fold noise next to the ladder. On this dataset, refitting the same linear
model across the five published shuffles moves the RMSE by ±0.05 MW. The random forest's
1.2 MW advantage over the linear model is roughly twenty times that noise, so it is real. A
0.03 MW advantage would not have been.
:::

### Case study: 50 million candidate predictors and one missing baseline

In 2009, a team from Google and the CDC published [a method for tracking influenza from search
queries](https://research.google.com/archive/papers/detecting-influenza-epidemics.pdf) in
*Nature*. The construction is worth reading closely, because it is a model-selection study of
a very familiar kind, run at an unfamiliar scale.

The team took weekly counts for **50 million search queries**, tested each one separately for
how well it fitted the CDC's influenza-like-illness rate in each of nine US regions, and kept
the top scorers. In the paper's words: "Each of the 50 million candidate queries in our
database was separately tested in this manner." They then chose how many of the top-ranked
queries to sum by measuring out-of-sample fit, and "Combining the N=45 highest-scoring queries
was found to obtain the best fit." That final model was fitted on weekly data from 2003 to
2007, 128 points per region, 1,152 in total.

So: 50 million candidate predictors, 1,152 observations. Roughly forty-three thousand
candidates per data point.

The authors were aware this was a hazard, and their own paper contains the evidence. The
queries just outside the top 45 "included topics like 'high school basketball,'" which
coincides with flu season without having anything to do with flu, and the paper notes that "A
steep drop in model performance occurs after adding query 81, which is 'oscar nominations'."
They also did the thing this session tells you to do: they held out a season. "The final model
was validated on 42 points per region of previously untested data from 2007-2008, which were
excluded from all prior steps," and on that untouched holdout it achieved "a mean correlation
of 0.97 (min=0.92, max=0.99, n=9 regions)."

That is a legitimate held-out test, correctly executed, reporting a genuinely excellent
number. And the system still failed.

By 2013, Google Flu Trends was, in the words of [Lazer, Kennedy, King and Vespignani's
post-mortem in *Science*](https://gking.harvard.edu/files/gking/files/0314policyforumff.pdf),
"predicting more than double the proportion of doctor visits for influenza-like illness (ILI)
than the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention." It was not a one-season fluke. The
authors document that GFT "has missed high for 100 out of 108 weeks starting with August
2011," and that the errors were autocorrelated and seasonal rather than random, which is to
say structured and therefore predictable and therefore correctable, had anyone been checking.

The diagnosis is the part that belongs in an engineering course. Lazer et al. call the
original construction "a particularly problematic marriage of big and small data," and
identify the mechanism precisely: "The odds of finding search terms that match the propensity
of the flu but are structurally unrelated, and so do not predict the future, were quite high."
The developers' own filtering of "seasonal search terms unrelated to the flu but strongly
correlated to the CDC data, such as those regarding high school basketball" was, they write,
the tell: "This should have been a warning that the big data were overfitting the small number
of cases, a standard concern in data analysis." The consequence was that "the initial version
of GFT was part flu detector, part winter detector," which is the most quotable description of
a confounded feature set anyone has written.

And then the baseline. Lazer et al. report that "lagged models significantly outperforming
GFT," and quantify it: "Mean absolute error (MAE) during the out-of-sample period is 0.486 for
GFT, 0.311 for lagged CDC, and 0.232 for combined GFT and CDC. All of these differences are
statistically significant at P < 0.05." A model built from the CDC's own already-published
numbers, on a two-to-three week lag, with 52-week seasonality terms, beat the search-query
system by 36%. The paper puts it flatly: "Even 3-week-old CDC data do a better job of
projecting current flu prevalence than GFT."

:::{admonition} What a practitioner should take from this
:class: tip

Three things, and the third is the one people miss.

First, **the ratio of candidates to observations is a design parameter, and you should know
its value.** Fifty million to 1,152 is an extreme, but a Kaggle-style hyperparameter sweep
over a 200-specimen materials dataset is the same arithmetic with smaller numbers.

Second, **a baseline that uses the target's own history is the one to beat**, and in
time-ordered problems it is often shockingly hard to beat. The GFT team never published a
comparison against lagged CDC data. Somebody else did it later, and the answer ended the
project.

Third, and least comfortable: **a correctly executed held-out test did not save them.** The
2007-2008 holdout was real, untouched, and produced 0.97. It could not detect the failure
because it was drawn from the same seasonal regime as the training data, and the failure modes
that eventually mattered (a non-seasonal H1N1 pandemic, and Google changing its own search
suggestions underneath the model) were outside the distribution the holdout sampled. A test
set answers exactly one question: *how would this have done on more data like the data I
already have?* When your deployment differs from that, the test set is silent, and the only
defences are monitoring and a baseline you keep running alongside in production.
:::

## Metrics, and the engineering cost of being wrong

A metric is a compression of every error your model makes into one number, and the compression
you choose declares what you think errors cost. That declaration is an engineering decision,
not a statistical convention, and the default is wrong more often than people notice.

**RMSE**, the root mean squared error, is the workhorse for physical regression. It is in the
same units as the target, which means an RMSE of 3.36 MW is directly comparable to a
tolerance, a control deadband, or a contractual limit. It penalises large errors
quadratically, so it is the right choice when one 20 MW miss is worse than ten 2 MW misses,
which is usually true when the error feeds a safety margin or a dispatch decision. **MAE**,
the mean absolute error, weights every megawatt equally and is therefore the right choice when
error cost is linear, for instance when you are billed per unit of imbalance. MAE is also far
more robust to outliers, which matters when your target came off an instrument that
occasionally drops out. If RMSE and MAE rank your models differently, the difference is
telling you that one model makes rare large errors and another makes frequent small ones, and
which of those you prefer is a question about the plant, not about the data.

**R²** is the fraction of the target's variance the model explains, and its usefulness is that
it is dimensionless and therefore comparable across problems. Its danger is exactly the same
property: it hides the units. An R² of 0.96 on the power-plant data sounds excellent and
corresponds to an RMSE of 3.36 MW, on a plant whose output ranges from 420 to 496 MW. Whether
3.36 MW is acceptable depends entirely on what consumes it, and R² will not tell you.
**Adjusted R²** corrects for the fact that plain R² never decreases when you add a predictor,
even a random one, by penalising the parameter count; it is worth reporting whenever you are
comparing models with different numbers of features on the same data.

### MAPE, and why it explodes

**MAPE**, the mean absolute percentage error, is the metric non-specialists ask for, because a
percentage feels intuitive. It divides each error by the true value, which means it is
undefined at zero and unstable near it, and the regime where the target approaches zero is
frequently the regime you care most about.

Remaining useful life is the clean example, and we have the numbers from
[L7](../l07/notes.md)'s C-MAPSS fleet. Take a model with a flat, perfectly uniform 15-cycle
error on every one of the 100 test engines. That is a single, constant, honest error, and
RMSE and MAE both report it as exactly 15 cycles. MAPE reports **39.5%**, and the single
engine whose true remaining life is 7 cycles contributes **214%** on its own. As the engine
approaches failure, which is precisely when the prediction has to be right, MAPE diverges. A
model tuned to minimise MAPE will learn to be very careful about nearly-dead engines and
sloppy about healthy ones, which may be what you want or may be exactly backwards, but it
should be a decision rather than a side effect of picking the metric that sounded friendliest.

### Residuals say what the summary cannot

No scalar metric can tell you that your errors are *structured*, and structured errors are
where the physics is hiding. Plot residuals against the fitted value and you find bias at the
extremes of the range, which usually means an unmodelled nonlinearity. Plot residuals against
each input and you find the variable the model is handling badly. Plot residuals against time,
where you have it, and you find drift, regime changes, and the sensor that was recalibrated in
March. A model with a beautiful RMSE and a residual plot that looks like a smile has a
correctable deficiency; a summary statistic will never tell you so.

### Fault detection, and the arithmetic of alarms

When the target is a class rather than a quantity, the metric problem changes shape and gets
harder, because engineering fault data is almost always imbalanced. Consider the
[UCI SECOM dataset](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/179/secom) from
[L5](../l05/notes.md): 1,567 semiconductor production runs with 590 process sensors each, of
which **104 runs failed**, a 6.6% failure rate. Accuracy is useless here by inspection: a
model that declares everything a pass scores 93.4%.

The two metrics that get reached for instead are **ROC-AUC** and **PR-AUC**, and under
imbalance they tell very different stories about the same predictions.

```{figure} figures/imbalance.png
:alt: Three panels sharing one model's predictions on the SECOM dataset. Left, an ROC curve with AUC 0.72 that looks like a working classifier. Middle, a precision-recall curve for the identical predictions with PR-AUC 0.18 against a base rate of 0.066. Right, a curve of wafers investigated per real fault against recall, rising from about 4 at 25% recall to 10 at 81% recall.
:width: 100%

One random-forest model, cross-validated on SECOM, summarised three ways. The predictions are
identical in all three panels; only the summary changes. Generated by
`figures/make_figures.py`.
```

The ROC curve reports an **AUC of 0.72**, comfortably above the 0.5 of a coin flip, and looks
like a working detector. The precision-recall curve on the identical predictions reports an
average precision of **0.18** against a base rate of 0.066. The reason for the difference is
structural rather than incidental: ROC's x-axis is the false positive rate, which divides by
the number of *negatives*, and when negatives outnumber positives fourteen to one, a large
absolute number of false alarms is a small false positive rate. Precision divides by the
number of *alarms raised*, which is what an operator experiences, and it does not get to hide
in a large denominator.

The right-hand panel converts this into the only units that matter for deployment: how many
things you have to go and look at to find one real fault. At 25% recall you investigate about
4 wafers per real fault. At 51% recall, 6. At 81% recall, **10**. That trade is the actual
product decision, and neither AUC number states it.

### Case study: 275 alarms in 11 minutes

The reason to take the alarm-burden axis seriously is that industry has already run this
experiment, expensively. On 24 July 1994 a lightning strike triggered a process upset at the
Texaco refinery at Milford Haven, and about five hours later an explosion injured 26 people
and caused around £48 million of damage. The Health and Safety Executive's information sheet
[*Better alarm handling*](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/chis6.pdf), which distils that
investigation, lists the contributing factors, and the first one is: "There were too many
alarms and they were poorly prioritised."

The number the HSE puts on it is worth memorising. "In the last 11 minutes before the
explosion the two operators had to recognise, acknowledge and act on **275 alarms**."

The same sheet states the budget those operators should have been working within, drawn from
the EEMUA guidance the HSE contributed to: "the long-term average alarm rate during normal
operation should be no more than one every ten minutes; and no more than ten displayed in the
first ten minutes following a major plant upset." Twenty-five alarms per minute against a
design target of ten in the first ten minutes.

Now do the arithmetic for a machine-learning fault detector, which is the part that should
change how you set a threshold. Suppose you deploy a model that scores 1,000 process tags once
a minute, with a false positive rate of 1%. That is a false positive rate most people would
call excellent. It generates ten false alarms per minute, which is one hundred times the
EEMUA normal-operation budget, from a model that is working as specified. The operator's
attention is a hard constraint with a published number attached, and it belongs in your metric
before you pick a threshold, not after the system is installed.

:::{admonition} What a practitioner should take from this
:class: tip

Write the cost matrix down before you choose a metric. What does a missed fault cost, in
scrap, downtime, or risk? What does a false alarm cost, in investigation time and in the
credibility the system spends every time it cries wolf? Those two numbers determine your
operating point, and once you have them the metric follows: expected cost per period, not
AUC.

Report PR-AUC alongside ROC-AUC whenever the positive class is under about 20% of the data,
and report the alert burden at your intended operating point in the units the operator will
count in. "PR-AUC 0.18" is for you. "Ten wafers to chase per real fault" is for the person who
has to decide whether to turn it on.
:::

## Choosing a cross-validation scheme

Cross-validation exists because a single train/validation split wastes data and gives a noisy
answer. **k-fold** partitions the data into k parts, trains k times holding out each part in
turn, and averages, so every row contributes to the estimate exactly once as validation data.
**Repeated k-fold** does this several times with different partitions and is the right default
when your dataset is small enough that the fold boundaries themselves move your answer around.

All of this rests on an assumption that is almost never stated out loud: that the rows are
**exchangeable**, meaning that any random partition into folds is as good as any other. When
that assumption fails, k-fold does not warn you. It returns a number, and the number is
optimistic.

Two structural failures matter in engineering data, and each has a matching splitter.

**Grouped data**, where rows share a physical unit: all cycles from one engine, all specimens
cast from one concrete batch, all points swept in one wind-tunnel run, all readings from one
patient. Here the question your model will actually be asked at deployment is "what about a
*new* unit," and a random k-fold never asks it, because most of every unit's rows are in the
training folds. **`GroupKFold`** holds out whole units.

**Time-ordered data**, where a row's position in time matters and using the future to predict
the past is meaningless. Here the correct scheme is **forward chaining**, which
scikit-learn implements as **`TimeSeriesSplit`**: train on the first *m* points, validate on
the next block, then extend the training window and repeat. Every validation block is strictly
after every training point, which is the only arrangement that resembles deployment.

### Measuring what the choice is worth

The power-plant rows are close enough to independent that plain k-fold is defensible, which
makes it the contrast case rather than the demonstration. For the demonstration, use the
[NASA Airfoil Self-Noise dataset](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/291/airfoil+self+noise),
1,503 measurements from a series of anechoic wind-tunnel tests reported in Brooks, Pope and
Marcolini's NASA RP-1218. Its structure is the point: each row is one *frequency* within one
tunnel configuration, and a configuration is a choice of chord length, free-stream velocity,
angle of attack, and suction-side displacement thickness. Group by configuration and the
1,503 rows collapse into **106 groups** of roughly 14 rows each.

```{figure} figures/cv-schemes.png

The mean and linear baselines are identical under both schemes; k-nearest-neighbours inflates by 1.28 times and the random forest by 1.52 times. Right, one wind-tunnel run's sound pressure level plotted against frequency, with three of nineteen points highlighted as held out by a random fold, each sitting between two retained neighbours.
:width: 100%

The same 1,503 rows, the same four models, two fold schemes. The right-hand panel is the
mechanism: a random fold holds out scattered points from inside a smooth frequency sweep the
model has otherwise memorised. Generated by `figures/make_figures.py`.
```

| model | KFold RMSE | GroupKFold RMSE | inflation |
|---|---|---|---|
| predict the mean | 6.90 dB | 6.91 dB | 1.00× |
| linear | 4.83 dB | 4.83 dB | 1.00× |
| k-NN, k=5 | 3.10 dB | 3.98 dB | **1.28×** |
| random forest | 1.76 dB | 2.69 dB | **1.52×** |

The random forest reports 1.76 dB under random k-fold and 2.69 dB when whole runs are held
out. Same data, same model, same code, and the number a paper would print is **52% too low**.

The right-hand panel of the figure is the more useful half, because it shows why. A single
wind-tunnel configuration is a smooth curve of sound pressure level against frequency, sampled
at about nineteen points. A random 5-fold holds out three or four of those points, each of
which sits between two points the model kept. Predicting them is interpolation along a curve
the model has already seen, which is not the task. The task is a configuration nobody ran.

Note also which models the leak reaches. The mean and linear baselines report the same number
either way, because neither has the capacity to memorise a particular run's curve. k-NN, which
is nothing but memorisation, inflates by 28%. The random forest, which is a great many
memorising trees, inflates most. This is the same lesson as [L7](../l07/notes.md)'s scaler
experiment, arriving from a different direction: **how much a leak costs you depends on
whether your model is capable of exploiting it**, so the absence of a gap is never evidence
that the split was sound.

One honest note about what did *not* change: the ranking survived. The random forest is still
the best model under `GroupKFold`, and the linear model is still worse. What broke was the
number, not the decision. That will not always be true, and it is a poor thing to rely on, but
it is what this dataset shows and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise.

:::{admonition} A caveat about this session's own dataset
:class: warning

The power-plant file contains a small scandal that is easy to miss. Open
`Folds5x2_pp.xlsx` and it has five sheets. They are not five different datasets: they hold
**the same 9,568 rows in five different orders**, which you can verify in three lines and
which the demo does. The readme explains why: "For comparability with our baseline studies,
and to allow 5x2 fold statistical tests be carried out, we provide the data shuffled five
times."

That is a defensible choice for reproducing a specific 5×2 cross-validation protocol. It also
means **the temporal order of six years of hourly measurements has been destroyed before the
data reached you.** You cannot plot the target against time. You cannot compute an
autocorrelation. You cannot run a `TimeSeriesSplit`. You cannot check the exchangeability
assumption that justifies the k-fold everybody runs on this dataset, including these notes.

And you have strong physical reason to think it fails. Ambient temperature at 2 p.m. and at
3 p.m. on the same day are nearly the same number, so consecutive hourly rows are close to
duplicates of each other, and a random k-fold will systematically place near-duplicate pairs
on opposite sides of the split. Whether that materially inflates the scores here is
unanswerable with the file as published, which is itself the finding.

The generalisable lesson is about how you publish data, not about how you consume it. **A row
ordering is metadata, and shuffling is destructive.** If you must ship a shuffled copy, ship
the index alongside it, or ship a timestamp. Otherwise every downstream user inherits a
validation question they have no way to answer.
:::

## The leakage catalogue, from the modelling side

[L7](../l07/notes.md) approached leakage from the feature-engineering side: every transform is
a statistic, and the question is which rows it was computed over. This session's view is from
the modelling side, where the same failure appears in four recognisable shapes.

### 1. Fitting a transform on all the data

The classic. Fit a `StandardScaler` on the full dataset, then cross-validate a model on the
scaled matrix. Every fold's "held-out" rows contributed to the mean and standard deviation
that transformed the training rows.

The module's teaching notes flag this as the bug to demonstrate, so it is worth reporting what
happens when you actually measure it on this dataset. It was measured, four ways:

| model | leaky CV RMSE | honest CV RMSE | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| `LinearRegression` | 4.5593 | 4.5593 | 0.00000 |
| `Ridge(alpha=100)` | 4.5757 | 4.5757 | −0.00005 |
| `KNeighbors(k=5)` | 3.8223 | 3.8213 | −0.00107 |
| `RandomForest` | 3.3712 | 3.3727 | +0.00145 |

(Measured on all 9,568 rows by `figures/make_figures.py`. The demo notebook repeats it on
the 7,654-row training split and gets the same answer to the same number of decimal places,
which is itself worth noticing.)

The largest effect across four models is **0.0015 MW**, against fold noise of ±0.05 MW. It is
not merely small, it is unmeasurable, and in two of the four cases the leaky pipeline scores
very slightly *better*.

This is the same result [L7](../l07/notes.md) got on C-MAPSS, for the same reason. Computing a
mean over 9,568 rows instead of 7,654 randomly chosen ones barely moves the mean. The leak is
real, it is bad practice, and it is worth zero here. Which is exactly why **you cannot audit
for leakage by looking at your metrics**: the bug is invisible in the number, so the only
reliable check is to read the code and find every `.fit()` call. Wrapping preprocessing in a
`Pipeline` closes it structurally, which is why you should do it, but do not expect the score
to thank you.

### 2. Target leakage from the future

A feature that would not be available at prediction time. In a plant setting this is usually
a downstream measurement: predicting a batch's yield using a quality-lab result that is
recorded after the batch completes, or predicting a fault using a maintenance-log field that
is only populated once the fault is diagnosed. These are the hardest to catch by inspection
because the column names sound innocent, and the tell is not statistical, it is temporal.
For every feature, ask: **at the instant the prediction is needed, does this value exist yet?**

### 3. Group leakage across the split

Measured above: 52% on the airfoil random forest. This is the one that hurts most in practice,
because no `Pipeline` catches it. The split happens before any pipeline sees the data.

### 4. Selecting on the test set

Every time you consult a score and change something, that score's data has been spent. Doing
this with the *test* set is obviously wrong; doing it with the *validation* set is normal
practice, and the cost is subtler. Because you report the minimum over your candidates, the
reported score is biased low even when nothing was done incorrectly.

**Nested cross-validation** is the standard fix. The inner loop selects the model; the outer
loop scores the entire selection procedure on data the inner loop never saw. The number it
gives you estimates the performance of *your whole workflow*, which is what you are actually
going to deploy.

How much is that worth? This is measurable, so it was measured, over a 36-candidate grid,
at eight training-set sizes.

```{figure} figures/selection-bias.png
:alt: Selection bias in megawatts plotted against training set size on a logarithmic axis. The bias falls from 0.19 MW at 80 rows to 0.08 at 160, 0.04 at 320, and reaches essentially zero by 2,560 rows, with a shaded band showing the fold-to-fold noise of the dataset.
:width: 100%

The difference between a nested cross-validation estimate and the best inner-CV score, against
the number of rows the selection study ran on. Both sides train on four fifths of what they are
handed, so the "the final model was refitted on more data" confound is removed and what remains
is selection bias. Generated by `figures/make_figures.py`.
```

The shape is the lesson. At **80 rows**, the winning configuration's validation score is
**0.19 MW better than it deserves**, roughly four times the fold noise. By **320 rows** the
bias has fallen inside the noise band. On all **9,568 rows** it is **−0.003 MW**, which is to
say unmeasurable, and marginally negative.

So nested cross-validation is not a universal tax you owe on every project. It is **small-data
insurance**, and the premium is a factor of k in compute. The judgement call is knowing which
regime you are in, and the rule of thumb the figure supports is that it is the ratio of
candidates to rows that matters, not either number alone. A 36-candidate grid over 9,568 rows
is safe. The same grid over the 106 wind-tunnel configurations of the airfoil dataset is not.

### The extreme case, and why it is in the reading

Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman devote section 7.10.2 of *The Elements of Statistical Learning*
to "the wrong way to do cross-validation," and it is worth reproducing because it shows the
same bug with the volume turned all the way up.

Generate 50 samples with 5,000 predictors, all of them pure independent noise, and a label
assigned by coin flip. There is nothing to learn: the true error rate of any classifier is 50%.
Screen the 5,000 columns down to the 100 most associated with the label, *then* cross-validate
a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier on those 100 columns, and you get a measured error rate of
**0.9%**. Move the identical screening step inside the cross-validation loop, so it is refitted
on each training fold, and you get **47.5%**, which is the coin flip it is.

The mechanism is the same as GFT's, minus the search queries: with 5,000 candidates and 50
observations, some columns will correlate with the labels by chance, and choosing them using
the labels of rows you are about to validate on hands the classifier the answer. The number of
candidates does not have to be fifty million for this to bite. It has to be large relative to
your sample size, and in a wide sensor matrix, it usually is.

## Bias, variance, and reading a learning curve

The last diagnostic is the one that tells you what to *do* next, which no single score can.
Plot training error and validation error against the number of training rows and the shape of
the two curves classifies your problem.

```{figure} figures/learning-curves.png
:alt: Three panels of training and validation RMSE against training-set size on the power plant data. Left, a linear model whose two curves have converged at 4.56 with essentially no gap. Middle, an unlimited-depth decision tree with zero training error and validation error near 4.6, a gap of 4.64. Right, a random forest with training error 1.25 and validation error still falling at 3.36, a gap of 2.10.
:width: 100%

Learning curves for three model capacities on the same 9,568 rows. The diagnosis is in the gap
between the curves and in whether the validation curve has flattened. Generated by
`figures/make_figures.py`.
```

The **linear model** converges to 4.557 MW on training data and 4.559 MW on validation data.
A gap of 0.002 MW means the model is doing equally badly on data it has seen and data it has
not, which is the signature of **underfitting**, or high bias. The model is not complex enough
to represent the relationship, and it does not matter how much more data you collect. The
action is more capacity: interaction terms, a nonlinear family, better features.

The **unlimited-depth decision tree** achieves exactly **0.000 MW** on training data and 4.64
on validation, a gap of 4.64 MW. Zero training error is not a triumph, it is a confession:
every leaf has been driven down to a single sample, so the tree has memorised the training
set. This is **overfitting**, or high variance, and the actions are the opposite ones: less
capacity (limit the depth), more regularisation, or more data. Note that the tree's validation
error does come down as data is added, from 6.07 MW at 153 rows to about 4.6 at 7,654, so
more data helps it, but the curve has flattened and the final point ticks back up. That
wobble is itself informative: a single unpruned tree is unstable enough that its validation
estimate moves around at this sample size.

The **random forest** sits at 1.25 MW training, 3.36 MW validation, a gap of 2.10 MW that is
substantial but stable, with a validation curve that is **still descending** at the right-hand
edge of the plot. That last observation is the actionable one. A validation curve that has not
flattened is telling you that collecting more data would still improve this model, which
converts a modelling question into a budgeting question your manager can actually answer.

:::{admonition} What a practitioner should take from this
:class: tip

The learning curve is the cheapest thing in this session and the most likely to be skipped.
Run it once per project, early, before you spend a week tuning. It answers "should I collect
more data or build a better model," and those two answers have wildly different costs.

Read it in this order: are the curves converged (bias-limited, get more capacity), is the gap
large and static (variance-limited, regularise), and is the validation curve still falling
(more data will pay)?
:::

## Where this pushes back

Everything above recommends more evaluation machinery, so it is worth being candid about what
that machinery costs and where it stops working.

**Cross-validation is expensive, and nested cross-validation is expensive squared.** A 5-fold
outer loop around a 5-fold inner loop over 36 candidates is about 900 model fits. For a random
forest on 9,568 rows that is minutes; for a neural network on a real dataset it can be days,
which is why the deep learning arc starting at L11 mostly does not do it.
The honest position is that k-fold is a small-data technique that stopped scaling, and at
large n a single well-constructed train/validation/test split with an explicit uncertainty
estimate is both cheaper and nearly as good.

**A cross-validation score is itself a random variable, and people report it as though it were
not.** Refitting the same linear model across the five published shuffles of the power-plant
data moves the RMSE across a range of 0.21 MW, with a standard deviation of 0.05. Any claim
that model A beats model B by less than about two of those standard deviations is not
supported by the experiment that produced it. Report the fold standard deviation next to the
mean, always, and treat differences inside it as ties. This is the reasoning behind the
**one-standard-error rule**: among models whose validation score is within one standard error
of the best, choose the simplest, because the ranking inside that band is noise and simplicity
is not.

**A single held-out test estimate is also one number with its own uncertainty**, and a
well-executed study reports that uncertainty rather than four significant figures. Bootstrap
the test predictions, or report the interval a normal approximation gives you, and let the
reader see that "3.36" is really "3.36 ± 0.1."

**Nested cross-validation estimates a procedure, not a model.** This surprises people. The
number it returns tells you how well *the workflow of selecting-and-fitting* performs on data
like yours; it does not tell you the performance of the specific model the workflow happened
to select this time. You still need a final fit, and you still need a held-out test if you
want a number attached to the artefact you are shipping.

**Every scheme here assumes the future looks like the past.** k-fold, `GroupKFold`,
`TimeSeriesSplit` and nested CV all estimate performance under the distribution your data was
drawn from. Not one of them can see a new operating regime, a replaced sensor vendor, a
different fuel, or a control system upgrade. Google Flu Trends had a correct held-out season
and was destroyed by Google changing its own search suggestions, which no resampling scheme
could have anticipated. The defences against that are not in this session: they are monitoring
and drift detection, which arrive in the MLOps session at L23, and the practice of keeping a
dumb baseline running in production so that the day your model stops beating it, you find out.

**And the metric is always a proxy for a cost you did not write down.** RMSE is not the money.
PR-AUC is not the operator's attention. Choosing a metric is choosing a model of what errors
cost, and that model deserves the same scrutiny as any other.

## In-class demo

The runnable notebook is [`l09-model-selection.ipynb`](l09-model-selection.ipynb). It fetches
and caches both UCI datasets on first run, so it needs no manual setup.

We build the power-plant ladder from predict-the-mean up to a random forest, scoring RMSE, MAE
and R² together so the disagreements are visible. We lock a test split at the top of the
notebook and do not touch it until the final cell. Then three leakage experiments, in order of
how much they cost:

The **scale-before-split** bug that the module names, measured honestly. It is worth about
0.001 MW here, and the discussion is why a real bug can be worth nothing, and what that implies
about auditing by metric.

The **screen-features-before-CV** bug from ESL 7.10.2, on pure noise, which reports 0.9% error
where the truth is 50%. The same class of bug as the first one, and a very different
consequence.

The **group leak**, on the airfoil data, which is the one you will actually meet: a random
k-fold reports 1.76 dB where `GroupKFold` reports 2.69 dB.

Along the way we verify by hand that the five sheets of the power-plant workbook are five
shuffles of one table, which is the moment students realise they cannot check the assumption
their k-fold rests on. Then we fix everything with a `Pipeline`, apply a one-standard-error
rule to select, touch the test set exactly once, and bootstrap the resulting number to attach
an interval to it.

Come with a prediction for one thing: whether the power-plant rows are i.i.d., and how you
would check.

## Summary

The output of a modelling study is a number that makes a promise about data you have not seen,
and this session is about the handful of habits that keep the promise honest. Build baselines
first and report a ladder, because on the power-plant data a single thermodynamic variable and
a straight line close 85% of the gap and the entire model tournament is fought over the
remaining 2.1 MW. Choose a metric that encodes what errors actually cost, remembering that
MAPE diverges where the target approaches zero and that ROC-AUC flatters a detector whose
operator will be chasing ten false alarms per real fault. Choose a cross-validation scheme
that matches the structure of the data rather than accepting the default, because holding out
whole wind-tunnel runs instead of scattered rows moves a random forest's reported error by
52%. Understand that leakage does not announce itself in your metrics: the scaler bug the
literature warns about is worth 0.0015 MW here, and the group split nobody mentions is worth
half your reported accuracy. Use nested cross-validation when the ratio of candidates to rows
makes selection bias real, which the measurement puts at a few hundred rows rather than a few
thousand. And read a learning curve before you tune anything, because it is the only cheap
diagnostic that tells you whether to buy more data or build a better model. L10 takes the
study you can now run correctly and makes it *auditable*: every run tracked in MLflow with its
parameters, metrics, data hash and git SHA, and a systematic hyperparameter search whose
results you can read without fooling yourself.
