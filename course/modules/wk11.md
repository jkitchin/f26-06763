## Lecture L20 — Reinforcement learning: learning to act from rewards (optional)
**Changed 2026-10-12:** expanded to a full 90-minute session (deck about 90 minutes, then the
notebook and questions in the last 20), with interactive figures and three worked examples.
Notes in `lectures/l20/`; the agents draft that used to live there became L19 (rewritten 2026-11-03; the draft is in git history).

**Objectives:**
- Write a small control problem as matrices, with where the system is as a vector of
  probabilities, each action as a matrix whose entries are the chances of moving between
  states, and the score of each move in a table, and take one step by multiplication.
- Explain how a rule for choosing actions is read from a table of action scores, and fill in
  that table by hand from rewards with the Q-learning update.
- Define reinforcement learning and its vocabulary: agent, environment, state, action,
  reward, policy, return, discount factor, value function, Q-function, episode, and
  exploration against exploitation (ε-greedy).
- Place reinforcement learning in the course's map of machine learning by the feedback the
  learner gets: a correct answer (supervised), samples of a distribution (generative), a score
  for one choice with no state (Bayesian optimization), or a score for a sequence of
  choices that change the system (reinforcement learning).
- Find the value of a state by adding up its discounted rewards, and the best action in each
  state by value iteration.
- Implement tabular Q-learning on tank level control and compare it with a tuned PID
  controller over several seeds.
- Say when reinforcement learning is the right tool and when a model with MPC, Bayesian
  optimization, or a tuned PID loop is, and read a published success claim for its baseline,
  seeds, sample count and simulator.

**Topics** (minutes of the 90; the bandit and cliff-walking sections were cut on 2026-11-04):
- **Why this matters** (5): the CoastRunners boat that scores 20% more than humans by circling
  a lagoon; the tokamak and the distillation column as the engineering successes.
- **Where reinforcement learning sits** (7): the kind of feedback each method gets, from
  Sutton & Barto's instructive against evaluative feedback. Supervised (L9, L10) is told the
  answer; generative (L16) learns a distribution; BO and active learning (L15) score one choice
  with no state; reinforcement learning scores a sequence of actions that change the state,
  with rewards that arrive late. RLHF as where it meets LLMs (L17).
- **The Markov decision process** (10): agent–environment loop, state, action, reward,
  transition, episode, policy π(a|s), return $G = \sum_k \gamma^k r_{t+k}$, V(s), Q(s, a), the
  Bellman equation, and credit assignment for a delayed reward.
- **The Markov decision process as matrices** (10): **added 2026-11-04.** On the three-level
  tank: the environment as one row-stochastic transition matrix $P_a$ per action plus a reward
  matrix $R$; an action acts by $p_{t+1} = p_t P_{a_t}$; a policy matrix $\Pi$ collapses them to
  a Markov chain $P_\pi$, $r_\pi$; policy evaluation is the linear solve
  $(I - \gamma P_\pi) V = r_\pi$ (worked by hand, 4.737 / 4.263 / 4.837); the max as the one
  nonlinearity and value iteration; model-based against model-free, Q-learning as the sample
  update of value iteration (Sutton & Barto 8.5); linear-in-features Q and DQN as learned
  features plus a linear head.
- **Q-learning on tank level control** (18):
  $Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\,[\,r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\,]$, the TD
  error, ε-greedy, off-policy; the update by hand on a three-level tank (a 3 × 2 Q matrix, six
  updates with every number, then convergence to Q* from value iteration); how a table chooses
  an action (argmax, the same last step as an L10 classifier, trained without labels); the PyCSE tank against a tuned PID over seeds; the action grid
  that cannot hold the setpoint; the 20-bin tank as five 20 × 20 matrices solved by value
  iteration (0.187 over eight starts; the failing seeds pick a trapping inflow in a rarely
  visited bin; counting the same 100,000 transitions into P-hat and solving gives 0.187 on every
  seed); changing the reward and watching the agent game it.
- **Deep reinforcement learning and the tools** (11): DQN and PPO; three ways a policy
  outputs an action (argmax over Q, a sampled softmax, a sampled Gaussian for continuous
  actions); a two-parameter Gaussian policy trained by REINFORCE on the tank, which finds the
  holding inflow; the standard libraries (Gymnasium, Stable-Baselines3, RLlib, CleanRL, TorchRL,
  TRL) and which algorithm takes which action space; Gymnasium's `step()` contract; DQN's 50
  million frames (about 38 days of play) against the human tester's 2 hours.
- **Case studies** (5): magnetic control of TCV (Degrave et al. 2022); Yokogawa and JSR's 35
  days on a distillation column (2022, vendor release); Zhou, Li & Zare 2017 (71% fewer steps
  than CMA-ES on simulated functions, no BO baseline); Google data-centre cooling (the 2016
  "40%" blog does not say RL, and the 2018 paper is a linear model with MPC).
- **Limitations and trade-offs** (7): sample cost, seed variance (Henderson et al. 2018),
  specification gaming (Krakovna et al. 2020), the simulation-to-plant gap, safety while
  exploring, and Recht's result that model-based control needs far fewer samples on LQR;
  a table of RL against MPC, PID and BO.

**Teaching notes:**
- Keep reinforcement learning tabular in class, with the update done by hand before the tank.
  Students ask how an RL agent "makes a decision" and whether it is a classifier, especially once
  continuous actions appear: answer with the argmax (discrete) against a sampled Gaussian
  (continuous), not with an algorithm zoo. The libraries get one table.
- Reward hacking is the failure mode to show, not just name: change the tank reward and watch
  the agent satisfy it the wrong way.
- Point students at the PyCSE chapter's "when not to use RL" section. For most engineering
  control problems with a good model, MPC or a tuned PID is the right answer.
- The PyCSE tank comparison is one run against hand-picked PID gains, and none of the five
  inflows holds the level at 5 (steady state is $h = (F/k)^2$: 0, 1, 4, 9, 16). Use both as
  the fair-baseline lesson rather than hiding them.
- Two famous numbers need care: the "40%" cooling figure is a 2016 blog post that never says
  reinforcement learning, and Zhou et al.'s "71% fewer steps" is on simulated functions
  against CMA-ES. Teach them as reading practice.
