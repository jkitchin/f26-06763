# Week 07 — Applied Engineering ML: Scientific ML and Uncertainty
**Arc:** Machine learning & deep learning (signature module) · **Lectures:** L13–L14

**Prerequisites (from prior weeks):** The complete ML workflow and MLflow tracking (Wk5);
PyTorch tensors, autodiff, training loops, and architectures for tabular/field/sequence
data (Wk6); leakage-aware, grouped, and time-aware splits (Wk4–Wk6). You should be able to
train and honestly evaluate both a classical baseline and a neural network on an
engineering dataset.

## Module learning objectives
- Put physics into a model in each of the main ways (a physics-informed loss, a network inside
  a differential equation, a neural DAE trained with its constraints enforced, a projection
  layer) and say what each one guarantees.
- Derive a neural ODE from a recurrent network, explain why some physics must be enforced
  (path constraints, DAEs), and contrast the sequential and simultaneous approaches to dynamic
  optimization.
- Explain what a **surrogate (emulator) model** is, and quantify its predictive **uncertainty**,
  distinguishing **aleatoric** from **epistemic** and **calibrating** it rather than trusting it.
- Produce prediction intervals with bootstrapped models, a Gaussian process and conformal
  prediction, and check their coverage on interpolation and extrapolation test sets.
- Integrate all of the above into a tracked, reproducible workflow.

Bayesian optimization, active learning and reinforcement learning, which use this
uncertainty to choose the next experiment, are week 8 (L15–L16).

## Lecture L13 — Scientific machine learning: PINNs, neural ODEs and neural DAEs
**Changed 2026-10-06.** This session used to cover surrogates, physics-informed models and
uncertainty quantification. It now covers scientific machine learning in depth; uncertainty
quantification moved to L14, alongside Bayesian optimization, which consumes it.

**Objectives:**
- Explain what a physics-informed neural network (PINN) minimizes, and why its physics holds
  only approximately.
- Build a PINN in JAX and compare its extrapolation with a plain network.
- Derive a neural ODE from a recurrent network by letting the time step go to zero, and train
  one with a differentiable ODE solver.
- Explain why some physics must be enforced rather than penalized, and what makes a model a
  differential-algebraic equation (DAE).
- Contrast the sequential and simultaneous approaches to training a dynamic model.
- Train a neural DAE with SiNDAE, and check that its predictions satisfy the constraints.
- Compare PINNs, neural ODEs, neural DAEs and projection layers: where the physics lives, and
  what each one guarantees.

**Topics:**
- Why physics: the fed-batch bioreactor (what it is, where it is used, its balances term by
  term), why the kinetics are learned (a multiscale mAb process; a CHO metabolic network, Wang,
  Harcum & Xie, CC BY), and three models trained on the same three batches predicting a new
  one: purely data-driven (negative substrate, worst), physics-informed (balances plus a learned
  μ, still negative substrate) and physics-enforced (a neural DAE, feasible). Gen 0 (surrogates,
  Sacks et al. 1989) and Gen 1 (surrogates with uncertainty, Jones, Schonlau & Welch 1998, the
  idea behind Bayesian optimization) from Victor's CMU seminar, framed for science and
  engineering, then Gen 2 (hybrid, Shah et al. 2025); SciML defined with four closure-term
  examples (pendulum, heat conduction, CSTR, tank).
- **PINNs** (Raissi et al.): the spring-mass as a draggable widget, its equation of motion
  derived from Newton's second law; data loss plus the equation's residual at collocation
  points (defined, with a figure), derivatives by autodiff (L11); the full JAX code of the
  notebook, annotated; the residual is a penalty, small but not zero ("favored, not enforced",
  after Victor's seminar); known failure modes (Krishnapriyan et al.; Wang, Teng & Perdikaris);
  one trained PINN is one trajectory, not the law of motion.
- **From RNNs to neural ODEs**, following Chen et al. (2018): the residual update (their Eq. 1)
  as an Euler step, the limit Δt → 0 (their Eq. 2); Euler steps on the spring-mass phase plane
  converging as the step shrinks; a neural ODE learns the vector field, not one discretization
  (their Fig. 1, redrawn); differentiable integrators (reverse-mode AD through the solver,
  L11); Kidger's work (thesis, neural CDEs, Diffrax and Equinox); the fed-batch
  neural ODE in Diffrax with the code that makes the plots; UDE (Rackauckas et al.) as an
  interchangeable name.
- **Physics-enforced machine learning**: Gen 2's guaranteed constraints; from a penalty to a
  hard constraint, and why (conservation, safety, bounds, quality, no λ); path constraints with
  chemical engineering examples (Souza et al.; Biegler 2017; Haugwitz et al.); DAEs, simply
  defined, on the tank manifold; then the neural DAE, defined on the spring-mass with the
  physics enforced (Victor's seminar slides 30 and 31), before the training methods. The DAE
  index is no longer taught.
- **Sequential vs simultaneous** dynamic optimization (Biegler): the sequential loop drawn with
  its loss landscape; a five-frame transition from one simulated curve to a discretized NLP;
  collocation on finite elements as "polynomial slope = model slope", without naming Lagrange
  polynomials, with Biegler (2007) for the equations; Pyomo.DAE and IPOPT; a measured comparison
  on parameter estimation; an animated side-by-side.
- **Training neural DAEs** (Lueg, Alves, Schicksnus, Kitchin, Laird & Biegler, 2026): the
  simultaneous training problem; smoother, pretraining and full NLP shown on the bioreactor; inference on a
  new batch against the sequential neural ODE; **SiNDAE** (CAPD slides 19 and 20, logos), its
  code on six slides that are the worked example's cells; grey-box networks and decomposition
  are no longer covered; the mAb glycosylation case (CAPD slides 15 and 18,
  and the CHO glycan slides' hybrid model and synthetic results, shared with permission;
  glycans as a critical quality attribute, Reusch & Tejada 2015). The predator-prey example is
  no longer used.
- **Constraints inside the network**: projection layers (a counterflow heat exchanger whose
  outlet temperatures must obey the energy balance, the architecture, the projection onto that
  linear balance, and a measured training run; KKT-hPINN, KKT-Hardnet, HardNet, Beucler et al.
  in the notes only), with a four-way comparison showing where the physics sits in each
  formulation. Optimization layers are no longer covered.
- **Not only neural networks**: one slide on other learners used with physics: Gaussian
  processes (Cruz-Bournazou et al. 2022), SINDy (Brunton, Proctor & Kutz 2016), symbolic
  regression (PySR, Cranmer 2023), and tree ensembles (Nemoto et al. 2025; OMLT).

**Live demo / in-class activity:** Two worked examples. `l13-pinn-jax.ipynb`: a plain network
and a PINN built from scratch in JAX and trained with Optax on ten points of a damped
spring-mass; extrapolation error and the residual. `l13-neural-dae.ipynb`: the fed-batch
bioreactor as a neural ODE in Diffrax (sequential), then as a neural DAE in SiNDAE, step for
step the SiNDAE example "Importing Measured Data, Fed-Batch Bioreactor" (three batches; product
balance diluted by F/V rather than the example's F/X); on a new batch run for 60 h the neural
ODE's substrate goes negative and the neural DAE's does not. Every code block on the slides is
a cell of these notebooks, in the lecture's order.

**Engineering framing / dataset:** A damped spring-mass (m = 1 kg, μ = 4 N·s/m, k = 400 N/m)
for the PINN and the sequential/simultaneous comparison; the fed-batch bioreactor of Lueg et
al. (2026, section 4.3), with Monod kinetics as the hidden truth, for the neural DAE; the
paper's tank manifold as a DAE example; a counterflow heat exchanger's energy balance for the projection layer. All data are generated by
`lectures/l13/figures/make_figures.py`.

## Lecture L14 — Uncertainty quantification for machine learning
**Changed 2026-10-08:** L14 is uncertainty quantification only. Bayesian optimization moved to
L15 and active learning to L16 (week 8), each with a full session. The current
`lectures/l14` draft (UQ, BO and AL together) is the starting point for L14 and L15.

**Objectives:**
- Distinguish aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty, and say which one more data reduces.
- Produce prediction intervals three ways (bootstrapped models, a Gaussian process, split
  conformal prediction) and say what each one assumes.
- Check calibration with a reliability diagram and interval coverage (PICP), on an
  interpolation test set and on an extrapolation test set.
- Choose a UQ method for an engineering model, knowing what each one costs and guarantees.

**Topics:**
- Why a single number is not enough: a surrogate's prediction feeds a decision (a mix
  design, a setpoint, a safety margin), and the decision needs an interval.
- **Aleatoric** (noise in the data, irreducible) vs **epistemic** (what the model does not
  know, reducible with data). One 1-D picture: a constant noise band, and a band that widens
  where the data run out.
- **Bootstrapped models**: resample the training set with replacement, fit B models, and use
  the spread of their predictions for the epistemic part; add the residual noise for a
  prediction interval. Bagging and the spread of a random forest as the same idea. **Deep
  ensembles** (Lakshminarayanan et al.) as the neural-network version (different random
  initializations, optionally bootstrapped data). Cost: B trainings.
- The **Gaussian process** posterior (from L9) as the analytic Bayesian option, and what its
  kernel assumes.
- **Conformal prediction**: split conformal (a held-out calibration set and a quantile of its
  residuals) gives a finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantee, assuming only
  exchangeable data. Conformalized quantile regression (CQR) for intervals whose width adapts
  to the input. What the guarantee does not cover: coverage for one input (it is marginal),
  and distribution shift. Tooling: MAPIE.
- Named in one line each, not taught: quantile regression (pinball loss), Monte Carlo
  dropout, Bayesian neural networks.
- **Calibration**: reliability diagram, prediction-interval coverage probability (PICP) against
  the nominal level, and interval width (sharpness); calibrate on held-out data.
- **Extrapolation** is where it matters: hold out a region of the input space, not random
  rows, and report coverage there separately. Every method above loses coverage under shift,
  conformal included.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** On the concrete strength dataset (L9's grouped split), build
intervals with a bootstrapped ensemble, a GP and split conformal prediction. Compare width
and coverage at 90%, draw the reliability diagram, then repeat on an extrapolation split
that holds out the lowest water/cement ratios and watch coverage fall.

**Engineering framing / dataset:** The concrete strength dataset (from L9): the strength of a
mix the plant has not poured yet, where an over-confident interval becomes an unsafe
structure. Dataset continuity with L9 and L15 is deliberate.

**Readings & resources:**
- Kendall & Gal, "What Uncertainties Do We Need in Bayesian Deep Learning for Computer
  Vision?" *NeurIPS* 2017 (arXiv:1703.04977): aleatoric vs epistemic.
- Efron & Tibshirani, *An Introduction to the Bootstrap* (1993): the bootstrap.
- Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel & Blundell, "Simple and Scalable Predictive Uncertainty
  Estimation using Deep Ensembles," *NeurIPS* 2017.
- Angelopoulos & Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free
  Uncertainty Quantification" (arXiv:2107.07511).
- Romano, Patterson & Candès, "Conformalized Quantile Regression," *NeurIPS* 2019
  (arXiv:1905.03222).
- Kuleshov, Fenner & Ermon, "Accurate Uncertainties for Deep Learning Using Calibrated
  Regression," *ICML* 2018 (arXiv:1807.00263).
- MAPIE documentation (scikit-learn-contrib): conformal prediction in Python.

## Teaching notes / common pitfalls
- Students conflate "the model gives an uncertainty" with "the uncertainty is correct."
  Insist on a calibration check (reliability diagram / PICP) — an uncalibrated interval is
  worse than none in a design loop.
- The aleatoric-vs-epistemic distinction is the conceptual crux of L14; anchor it with
  the GP demo (variance grows away from data = epistemic) so it is not just vocabulary.
- Conformal's guarantee is marginal and assumes exchangeable data. Students read it as "every
  interval is right" and as "it survives extrapolation"; the extrapolation split shows neither
  holds.
- PINNs are seductive but finicky; for a 2-week timeline, a *soft physics penalty* or a
  hard positivity/monotonicity constraint on a standard net is a far safer "physics-informed"
  choice than a full PDE-residual PINN. Steer final projects accordingly.
- L13: students conflate "the residual is small" with "the physics holds". The spring-mass
  PINN's residual is 0.7 N at its collocation points and 5.3 N between them; the fed-batch
  neural DAE keeps S ≥ 0 but its biomass is further from the truth than the integrated
  model's. Feasible is not accurate, and both points are on the slides.
- SiNDAE 1.0.2's built-in `FedBatchBioreactorProblem` writes the product balance with F·P/X
  instead of the paper's F·P/V (Eq. 30b). The L13 notebook defines its own problem with F·P/V;
  tell students who reach for the built-in class.
- Extrapolation is where surrogates fail and where design loops push them. Always hold out a
  region of the design space, not just random rows, and report extrapolation error
  separately.
- **The live plant stream is available as a bring-your-own dataset, under one framing.** The
  Tennessee Eastman publisher that A3 collects from runs continuously and anonymously all
  semester, and it fits the miniproject as a **soft sensor**: predict a product-composition analyser
  (`XMEAS_40_product_G`, which arrives up to twelve plant minutes stale and holds still in
  between, exactly like a lab assay) from the thirty-four continuous channels that arrive
  every sample. That is what a surrogate is when a plant builds one, uncertainty is a real
  requirement rather than a rubric item because the plant acts on the value between assays,
  and the extrapolation hold-out the miniproject demands is already in the data, since a disturbance
  episode is a regime the quiet stretch does not cover. Two things to check before approving
  it: the student needs hours of collection or a local replay rather than A3's ten minutes,
  and they must deduplicate on `(tag, sourceTimestamp)` first, because the latched target
  otherwise trains on repeated rows and reports an optimistic error. Full write-up, including
  what else the stream could carry, is in `stream/README.md` under "Reusing it later".
