## Lecture L7 — Features for time-series models
**The session in one sentence:** a step test means taking a unit off setpoint and the archive is already on disk, so this session gets the time constant and the gain out of the archive instead, by putting one column back in the table.

**Objectives:**
- Explain why a dynamic process needs its own past in the feature table, and a steady-state measurement does not.
- Build a feature table from a sensor log in which every value in a row was knowable at that row's timestamp, dropping the rows whose past lies outside the record.
- Convert the two coefficients of a fitted first-order model into a time constant and a gain, and say whether they are physically plausible.

**Topics:**
- **One example, all session.** One loop, start to finish: the A and C feed of Tennessee Eastman, `xmv_4` (the valve) against `xmeas_4` (the flow it sets). `xmeas_4` is a FLOW, in kscmh, not a temperature. **Do not introduce a second dataset.** Earlier drafts opened on beer sales and then moved to a stirred tank; cold readers reported the session read as a demand-forecasting lecture for its first seven minutes. There is no tank and no beer anywhere in the material now.
- **Why the session exists, stated before anything else.** Feature engineering has no self-check: a model fits, reports R-squared 0.94, and that number is just as high for a table that used a future value, grouped on the wrong column, or leaned on a channel that was only repeating itself. So the session picks a loop whose answer *is* checkable, because a first-order process converts its coefficients into minutes and flow-per-percent, which a plant engineer can judge. The step-test-versus-logged-data trade is the *second* motivation, not the first. An earlier draft led with the trade and a cold reader could not say what the lecture was for until slide 20.
- **Continuous-time and discrete-time models.** The spine, and the thing that justifies the whole feature discussion. Every model they have written is continuous (`tau dy/dt = -y + K u`); their data is discrete, one row per three minutes. Discretize with **zero-order hold**, which is free on a plant because the control system writes one valve position per scan: the integration is exact and yields `y[t+1] = a y[t] + b u[t]` with `a = exp(-dt/tau)`, `b = K(1-a)`. Verified symbolically and numerically (matches a tight ODE solve to 2e-14; Euler's coefficient would be `1 - dt/tau = 0.717` against the true `0.7535`). **Then read the difference equation as a table specification:** it names exactly two columns, so the features are derived rather than guessed. Name **ARX(1,1)** here, at the equation. Invert to `tau = -dt/ln(a)`, `K = b/(1-a)`. The **lag plot** shows `a` as a slope before any fit.
- **Data leakage.** Defined as IBM defines it: training on information that would not be available when the prediction is due. Taught through the **credit card chargeback** example (a column filled in weeks after the fraud is decided), then the same shape in our own file: `faultNumber`, written down after the run, and the slow analysers, whose 15-minute result is stamped at 14:00 but not available until 14:15. Then IBM's two categories: **target leakage** (a table property, ours) and **train-test contamination** (needs a held-out score, so it is named and handed on). **Use "leakage", never "realizability".** Realizability is a real control term for whether a transfer function is proper, so it collides, and the course already teaches this as leakage in L9.
- **Run boundaries and the group key.** The file holds 192,000 rows that are 400 separate experiments, identified by the pair `(faultNumber, simulationRun)`, not by `simulationRun` alone. Measured: `shift(1)` leaves 1 null and fixes 0 of 399 boundaries, `.over("simulationRun")` leaves 20 and fixes 19, `.over("faultNumber", "simulationRun")` leaves 400 and fixes all. The middle case is the dangerous one because the nulls make it look like it worked, so teach **count the nulls against the number of experiments**. An earlier draft shipped `.over("simulationRun")` as correct; it was not. **This is notes-and-demo only: it has no slide any more.**
- **Common data preparation operations, rapid fire, about eight minutes.** All on the same file. **(a)** Build the clock, because the file ships `sample` as an integer and has no timestamps, then check the intervals with `pl.col("ts").diff().value_counts()`, since `shift(1)` reaches back one *row* and not three minutes. **(b)** `upsample` back onto a grid and decide the fill per column: carry the valve forward (a control system really does hold its last position, which is zero-order hold again) and leave the measurement null, keeping a mask either way. **(c)** `rolling_std` with the same `.over(GROUP)` as the shift. **(d)** The channel screen, `pl.col("^xmeas.*$").diff().eq(0).mean()`, which on one run returns 19 of 41 channels flat on more than 40 % of rows, at exactly 0.500 and 0.800: the 6- and 15-minute analysers from Downs and Vogel table 5 on a 3-minute grid. Cut (c) first if the block runs long.
- **Sampling interval against the time constant.** Three-minute samples against a 10.6-minute time constant is about **three and a half samples per time constant**, which is why consecutive rows barely differ and why guessing "no change" is already right to within 9 % of the channel's spread three minutes out.
- **Multiple lags and the regression vector.** One lag is a starting point, not a rule. The general ARX form, the **regression vector** phi(t), the **design matrix** Phi, and `y = Phi theta`, which `lstsq` solves at any width. Measured on this loop: adjacent lag columns correlate at **0.927** (0.996 on reactor pressure), so Phi goes near-singular. What that does, measured: the one-lag `y[t]` coefficient of **0.753** becomes **0.417** at three lags, with the remainder landing on `y[t-1]` and `y[t-2]`. **Do not claim the coefficients blow up or alternate in sign on this data. They do not.** The real cost is that no single coefficient converts to a time constant any more.
- **Model structures: ARX, NARX, state space, RNN.** The ladder, in one notation: ARX(1,1) is the discrete first-order lag; widen it for ARX(na,nb); add dead time for FOPDT; make the function nonlinear for NARX; write it with a vector state for state-space; make that update nonlinear for an RNN, and add gates for an LSTM. The payoff line: `y[t+1] = a y[t] + b u[t]` and `h[t+1] = tanh(W h[t] + U u[t] + b)` are the same line of arithmetic. Every model on that ladder eats the same feature table, which is the argument for spending a session on the table.
- **Limitations: what the data cannot answer.** Two failures that arrive as plausible numbers. **The valve that never moved**: rank drops to 1, gain returns 0.000, and no feature engineering or bigger model recovers it because the information was never collected. Name **persistent excitation** once, after the concrete case. A single step is enough; only a genuinely constant input fails. **Closed-loop identification**: fitting a controlled loop returns a coefficient with the wrong sign, because the controller moved the valve *because* the measurement moved. Under tight control with no external excitation the direct estimate is pulled toward the negative inverse of the controller, so you identify your own PID tuning. **This is the session's required case study and must never be cut.**
- **One practical warning worth keeping.** `lstsq` needs an explicit `rcond`. A constant column is exactly zero only after mean-centering, and at the default tolerance the solver divides by rounding dust and returns a gain of nine hundred million instead of zero.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** ONE notebook, 27 cells, deliberately short, and it does **not** simulate anything. Four steps: load one run and build the clock; build an ARX regressor matrix twice (n_a=1,n_b=1 then n_a=3,n_b=2) and **print both matrices as labelled tables before fitting**, because seeing phi(t) laid out is the thing prose cannot do; solve with `lstsq` and convert (`a = 0.7529`, `b = 0.0322`, so tau = 10.6 min and K = 0.1304); then freeze the valve and refit, and watch the gain go to zero. An earlier version ran 63 cells over two datasets and did not fit the session.

**The demo no longer writes a Parquet file.** It used to hand L8 a 191,600-row feature table. It does not any more. See the L8 block below.

**Objectives:**
- Describe the supervised setup: a table of features, a target, and parameters fitted from data.
- Distinguish parameters learned by fitting from settings you choose yourself.
- Explain why shuffling the rows of a time series inflates the reported result, and split the data so it does not.
- Compare a fitted model against a baseline and say whether it earned its place.

**Topics:**
- **What machine learning is**, briefly and concretely: a function fitted from data rather than derived from physics, a table of features and a target, `fit` and `predict`, and the difference between numbers the fitting chooses and numbers you choose. This is the floor for a room that has never called `.fit()`, and it is kept to that. Bias and variance, learning curves, and the argument about which metric to use are L9's. **Note that L7 already had them call `lstsq` on a design matrix, so the leap is smaller than it looks: what is new here is the vocabulary and the idea of a held-out score, not the mechanics of fitting.**
- **Why time-ordered rows cannot be shuffled.** RECALL then teach: L7 already named this as *train-test contamination* and said the fix is to split on time, so do not re-motivate it. What L8 owns is the mechanism and the measurement. Autocorrelation has a job here: neighbouring rows of a plant record are near-copies, so a random split puts a row's own neighbours in the training set and the reported score describes the sampling rate rather than the model. L7 measured the raw numbers on this file already (adjacent lags at 0.927 on `xmeas_4`, 0.996 on reactor pressure), so the plot of correlation against lag in minutes extends a number the room has seen. Then forward chaining, which scikit-learn calls `TimeSeriesSplit`. **L8 owns this; L9 must not reintroduce it.**
- **Baselines, as a ladder.** Predicting no change is L7's **naive forecast**, named there and measured on reactor pressure (9 % of the channel's spread at 3 minutes, 68 % at 30). Recall the name and the numbers rather than redefining them. Note that L7 cut the figure and the slide for this, so the numbers live only in L7's notes and its demo; check they are still there before relying on the room having seen them. Then a model using only the channel's own past. Then one that also uses the valve. Each rung rules something out, and the middle rung is the one that says whether the manipulated variables earned their place in the table at all.
- **Fitting the first model on a feature table.** RECALL, then extend. L7 already did least squares on a design matrix, already converted `a` and `b` into a time constant and a gain, and already named **ARX(1,1)** and **NARX**, so none of that is new. What is new in L8 is the held-out score: the same fit, now judged. The natural step up is a different channel, more lags than L7 used, and the first honest evaluation of whether the extra lags earned their place, which L7 explicitly could not answer because it had no held-out number. **L7 measured the trade that motivates this**: going from one lag to three moved the `y[t]` coefficient from 0.753 to 0.417 and cost the ability to read a time constant off it. L8 is where that trade finally gets settled with evidence.
- **Summarizing a window, as a feature set rather than as a method call.** L7 now shows `rolling_std` in its rapid-fire block, so L8 does not re-teach the syntax. What L8 owns is the trade: a short window still fits noise, a long one smears out the onset you were trying to catch. Build a real window feature set (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum over two window lengths), put it next to the lag-only table, and report whether it earned its place. **L12 and A6 both require hand-crafted window features, and this is the session that teaches choosing them.**
- **Fitting transforms on training rows only.** `Pipeline` and `ColumnTransformer` so the rule is structural rather than remembered, and saving the fitted transformer as an artifact. Note that plain least squares gives the same answer whether or not you rescale, so this is an argument about where a transform is fitted, not a claim that scaling changes this model's answer.
- **Predicting further than one step.** One step ahead against letting the model run on its own predictions, and why a model that looks excellent one step ahead can wander off when it has to simulate. Report errors in the units of the measurement; which metric to prefer is L9's argument.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** Build the feature table first (see the note at the top of this block: L7 no longer writes one). Then fit least squares on a different channel and with more lags than L7 used, so this is visibly a step up rather than a repeat, and read the coefficients back as a gain and a time constant. Run the ladder (no change, own past only, own past plus valve) and report each as an error in the channel's own units, so the comparison is visible on a projector in a way a number near 0.99 is not. Then the same model on a random row split against a time-ordered split, reported in the same units. Close by letting the model run on its own predictions and watching the curve that looked excellent come apart.

## Teaching notes / common pitfalls
- The sign of the shift is the first thing to get wrong. `shift(1)` puts the previous row's value on this row; `shift(-1)` reads the future. Show both with timestamps printed, and give students the leakage sentence to say out loud for every column they build.
- Subtract the mean, the way they already do in control. On a channel with a large operating-point offset, skipping it returns a badly wrong `a`, and it will happen in the live demo if nobody checks.
- The rows at the head of each run are not missing data. Their past lies outside the record, so drop them. `fill_null(0)` invents a reactor at zero degrees.
- Students will add lag columns until the table is wide. Lags of a smooth signal correlate above 0.99, so the coefficients come back enormous with alternating signs while the predictions still look fine. Print the correlation matrix once and let them see it. Adding a rolling mean whose window fits inside the lag depth makes the problem worse, and `lstsq` picks one of infinitely many answers without warning anyone.
- `faultNumber` in the table is the answer to the question being asked. It will happen.
- The loop plot needs an input that moves up and back down. A monotone ramp gives a displaced curve, not a loop, so check the chosen window before class.
- Composition analysers report on a slow cycle and hold their previous value in between, so a lag-1 difference on those channels is exactly zero most of the time. This is now a slide, a figure and a demo cell, item (d) of the four-operations block, and it is the most process-specific thing in the session: the flat fractions come out at exactly 0.500 and 0.800, which are the 6- and 15-minute analyser cycles on a 3-minute grid. Let the room read the two plateaus off the bar chart before you say what they are.
- L7 fits one least-squares solve and reports a time constant. L8 fits models and reports errors. If L7 starts reporting scores it has taken L8's session, and students meet metrics before anyone has defined one.
- Both sessions will run long. L7's abort order: cut the coefficient-perturbation cell first, then the persistence slide and cell (email the number, L8 only needs the value). Inside the four-operations block, item (c) goes first and is replaced by its one sentence. Do not cut the lag plot: it is the cheapest diagnostic in the course and it is the visual derivation of the whole session. Never cut the reversed-causality block; it is the best ten minutes of the week.
