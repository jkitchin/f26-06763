# Week 05 — The ML Workflow, End to End
**Arc:** Machine learning & deep learning · **Lectures:** L9–L10

**Prerequisites (from prior weeks):** A reproducible `uv` project with a lockfile (Wk1);
ability to load and query engineering data from DuckDB/Parquet (Wk2); pandas/Polars
pipelines with schema validation (Wk3); leakage-aware feature engineering for time series
(L7). And L8, which this week builds directly on: supervised learning as `fit`/`predict` on a
feature table, parameters against hyperparameters, the held-out score, the mean and
persistence baselines, ridge with `StandardScaler` inside a `Pipeline`, `TimeSeriesSplit`, and
splitting by run when there are many series. L9 recalls these by name and does not teach them
again.

## Module learning objectives
- Frame an engineering prediction problem as regression or classification, compare model
  families on it, and choose a metric that matches the engineering cost of error.
- Design and run **cross-validation** correctly for i.i.d., grouped, and imbalanced
  engineering data, avoiding the leakage traps that inflate offline scores.
- Perform an honest **model-selection study**: compare model families and hyperparameters
  on validation data, then report a single held-out test estimate with uncertainty.
- Instrument every training run with **MLflow** — parameters, metrics, artifacts, and the
  exact data/code version — so results are reproducible and comparable.
- Run a systematic **hyperparameter search** (grid, random, and Bayesian/Optuna) and read
  the results without fooling yourself (multiple-comparison and selection bias).

**The session in one sentence:** every supervised model in scikit-learn is trained and
scored the same way, so the decisions that matter are the split, the features and the
metric, and a split that ignores how the rows were collected can rank the models in the
wrong order.

**Objectives:**
- Frame an engineering question as regression or classification, and name its samples,
  features and target.
- Split data into training, validation and test sets, and explain why the test set is used
  only once.
- Fit and compare linear, decision tree, neural network and Gaussian process models through
  scikit-learn's common `fit` and `predict` interface, on a table of experiments and on a NARX
  table.
- Write the training of a linear model, a neural network and a Gaussian process as an
  optimization problem, and say what each one optimizes.
- Use k-fold and grouped cross-validation, validation curves and learning curves to diagnose
  underfitting and overfitting and to choose between models.

**Topics** (in the order of the notes and the deck):
- **The examples in this session.** Water at constant density (NIST), surfactant viscosity
  (Rehage and Hoffmann 1988), concrete strength (Yeh 1998), and the Tennessee Eastman process
  (L5, L8), each introduced once with a figure, plus one synthetic set ($y = x^{1/3}$ plus noise).
- **Types of machine learning.** Supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning (the
  Peng et al. 2021 schematic, CC BY 4.0); classification against regression; the vocabulary
  (sample, feature, target, model, training/test set, prediction, residual), then every example
  named in it. What changes from L8: most rows have no time order, so a shuffled split is
  allowed, unless rows share a unit; the TEP rows are still split by run.
- **The workflow.** Feature engineering (powers, logs, and the lagged NARX columns; anything
  fitted to data is fitted after the split), splitting, model selection, validation. Training,
  validation and test sets; the test set is touched once. Regression metrics with their names.
  "Where is the training here?"
- **Training is an optimization problem** (the PSE view), before the families: a general NLP,
  then the surrogate $y = f(x;\theta)$ with $\theta$ as the decision variables and the loss as
  the objective, then how an optimizer moves (one update, gradient descent, SGD, Adam and L-BFGS,
  with scikit-learn's own advice on which to use) and a figure of four optimizers on one
  least-squares problem. PINNs, neural ODEs and neural DAEs are L13's; the deck mentions them
  once, in a "spoiler" slide, at Victor's request.
- **Regression model families**, each ending with what its training solves:
  - linear regression and feature engineering (the NIST water example: a third-degree
    polynomial scores R² = 0.99999 on training data and is wrong outside it);
  - regularization, ridge against lasso, the live α slider, and regularization as a penalty
    (ESL 3.4.1);
  - four families and why more than one (a strengths and weaknesses table, and Wolpert's no
    free lunch theorem);
  - decision trees (splits that minimize MSE, grown greedily, NP-complete to do optimally);
  - neural networks: three tanh units written out, then in matrix form, the drawn network and a
    deep one, ReLU and "why neural" (interactive), hyperparameters, training (ten random
    starts), why inputs must be scaled ("just math, not magic");
  - Gaussian processes: a distribution over functions, predictive mean and variance, kernels;
    training written next to the network's (kernel hyperparameters, bounds, L-BFGS-B), the
    negative log marginal likelihood as misfit plus complexity penalty, and the length-scale
    trade-off on the surfactant data (interactive);
  - what each family optimizes, side by side;
  - **NARX, to glue to L7 and L8:** L8's direct forecaster (reactor pressure, 10 lags plus
    11 valves, 30 minutes ahead) fitted as a ridge ARX, an NN-NARX and a GP-NARX with the same
    `fit`/`predict`. Scale the target (L7's deviation variables). They tie;
  - the four families on concrete, with the baseline named as one.
- **Validation and choosing a model.** k-fold cross-validation and `GroupKFold`, with the
  ranking flip on concrete.
- **Model capacity, overfitting and learning curves**, its own section: capacity, under- and
  overfitting, the bias-variance decomposition (ESL eq. 7.9), validation curves (error against
  a capacity knob), learning curves (error against data) with the table of what each pattern
  means and what helps. The notes also choose and test once; the deck leaves that to the demo.
- **Limitations.** Extrapolation on the water data; where scikit-learn stops (the GP's O(N³) wall, 167 GB for all of L8's rows; the
  course toolchain, PyTorch and JAX, cited through the syllabus). The session ends on "but wait,
  we didn't discuss the hyperparameters?", written as the bilevel problem, and leaves it open
  without naming L10.

**Datasets (chosen on purpose):**
- **NIST water at 1000 kg/m³** (21 points, 0 to 100 °C; the isochore to 300 °C for
  extrapolation) and **surfactant viscosity** (16 experiments, Rehage and Hoffmann 1988, from
  Victor's F25 repository): the one-input examples for feature engineering, regularization, trees,
  and Gaussian processes, including the length-scale trade-off.
- **UCI Concrete Compressive Strength** (Yeh 1998; 1,030 rows, 7 ingredients in kg/m³ and
  age in days → strength in MPa; CC BY 4.0) for regression. It is small enough for a GP and
  it is **grouped**: the 1,030 rows are **428 distinct mixes**, 182 of them tested at more
  than one age, holding **76% of the rows**, and 25 rows are exact duplicates. Measured
  (`lectures/l09/figures/make_figures.py`, test set of 86 whole mixes held out, 5-fold CV on
  the other 835 rows):

  | model | KFold RMSE (MPa) | GroupKFold RMSE (MPa) |
  |---|---|---|
  | baseline: predict the mean | 16.8 | 16.8 |
  | linear | 10.6 | 10.7 |
  | linear, with physics features (log(age), w/c) | 7.25 | 7.43 |
  | decision tree (full depth) | 6.92 | 9.42 |
  | MLP (16 tanh, scaled) | 5.96 | 8.02 |
  | GP (RBF per input + white noise) | 5.81 | 7.17 |

  The random split ranks the tree above the engineered straight line; the split by mix ranks
  it far below. Two physics features (log of age; water/cement ratio, Abrams 1918) close
  most of the gap to the GP. Test once on the held-out mixes: GP 5.39 MPa.
- **Tennessee Eastman fault-free file, NARX** (L8's table: `xmeas_7`, 10 lags plus the 11
  valves, h = 10, train runs 1-300 = 144,300 rows, test runs 401-500). Test RMSE: persistence
  5.82, mean 7.57, ridge ARX 4.71, NN-NARX (32 tanh, target scaled) 4.73, the same network with
  the target unscaled 7.57 (it predicts the mean), GP-NARX on 1,000 rows 4.76 (ARX on the same
  rows 4.76; 94.8% of test points within 2 std; about 30 s), on 4,000 rows 4.72 (about 6 min).
  A GP on all rows would need a 167 GB kernel matrix.
- **x^(1/3) toy, ten random starts** of the 3-tanh network: training SSE 0.0796 to 0.1177
  (1.48x), test R² 0.920 to 0.950, the non-convexity picture for the optimization section.

**Objectives:**
- Fit and compare logistic regression, decision tree, neural network and Gaussian process
  classifiers, and say what each one minimizes.
- Choose metrics that match the cost of an error, including precision and recall when one class
  is rare.
- Use stratified k-fold when one class is rare, and test a classifier on classes it was not
  trained on.
- Instrument a training script so every run is logged, comparable, and reproducible.
- Run grid, random, and Bayesian hyperparameter search and interpret the results honestly.
- Register a selected model with its metrics, params, and data lineage.

**Topics:**
- **Classification** (moved from L9). The moons dataset defined; logistic regression with a figure; what each
  family minimizes for regression against classification (Victor's F25 framing: Gini impurity,
  softmax and cross-entropy, a latent GP with a link); decision regions on the moons; fault 14 on
  the plant; the confusion matrix with TP, FP, FN and TN spelled out, precision, recall, F1, and
  the "always normal" baseline; stratified k-fold, with the class fractions defined.
- Why tracking exists: the "which run produced this number?" problem. What to log for every
  run — hyperparameters, git commit SHA, dataset version/hash (tie back to Wk4 data
  versioning), random seed, environment (`uv.lock`), metrics per epoch/fold, and artifacts
  (fitted pipeline, plots, feature importances).
- MLflow concepts: **experiments** vs. **runs**, `mlflow.log_param` / `log_metric` /
  `log_artifact`, autologging for scikit-learn and PyTorch, nested runs for CV folds and
  search trials, the MLflow **Model Registry** and model URIs, and the tracking UI
  (`mlflow ui`). Local file-store vs. a tracking server.
- Hyperparameter search strategy: grid vs. random (Bergstra & Bengio: random beats grid in
  high dimensions) vs. Bayesian/TPE with **Optuna**; search spaces, budgets, pruning of
  bad trials, and multi-fidelity ideas (successive halving / Hyperband). Logging every
  trial to MLflow so the search itself is auditable.
- The statistics of selection: with enough trials some model "wins" by chance. Report the
  validation distribution, use a single held-out test at the very end, and prefer the
  simplest model within one standard error of the best (the 1-SE rule).
- Reproducibility hygiene: seeding, deterministic splits, and re-running a logged run from
  its recorded config.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** Two notebooks. `l10-classification.ipynb` (moved from L9):
the moons and their four decision regions; the TEP classifier, its confusion matrix and its recall
on faults it never saw. Then `l10-tracking-search.ipynb`: wrap the Week-5 power-plant models in MLflow. Run an
Optuna study over random-forest and gradient-boosting hyperparameters with nested runs,
open `mlflow ui`, sort by validation RMSE, and use the parallel-coordinates and
contour plots to read which hyperparameters mattered. Register the winner and show loading
it back by model URI for a fresh prediction.

**Datasets for the classification half (moved from L9):**
- **Moons** (`make_moons(300, noise=0.25, random_state=0)`, Victor's L9 example) for decision
  regions: logistic 0.867, tree (depth 3) 0.911, MLP 0.933, GP 0.967 test accuracy.
- **Tennessee Eastman, the miniproject's two files** (fault-free runs 1-500; faults 1-20,

## Teaching notes / common pitfalls
- **L9: do not spoil the miniproject.** Its evidence script checks that faults **3, 9 and
  15** are reported as not detected. L10's TEP classifier never trains or tests on those
  three, and no per-fault table in L9 covers all twenty faults. Keep it that way.
- **L9: the ranking flip is the concrete lesson.** Under a random `KFold` the full-depth tree
  (6.92 MPa) beats a straight line with two physics features (7.25); under `GroupKFold` on
  the mix the tree is 9.42 and the line 7.43. The line barely moves because it cannot
  memorize a mix; the tree memorizes the 28-day row and "predicts" the 56-day row of the same
  mix. Say it that way, and point out that L8's split by run is the same idea without time.
- **L9: the GP tested once (5.39 MPa) comes in below its grouped CV score (7.17).** That is
  one draw of 86 mixes, not a sign that something is wrong; the demo's markdown says so.
  Across ten different test draws the GP's test RMSE ranges as measured in
  `figures/make_figures.py`.
- **L10: fault 14 is the moons picture on real data.** The mean of `xmv_10` does not move, the
  spread grows fourteen-fold, and 45% of faulty samples land on each side of the normal band.
  A linear boundary cuts one side; the tree's first two splits (39.13 and 43.05) are a band.
- **L10: GP classification does not run on the TEP table** (172,500 rows; O(N³)). Say so, it is
  the GP weakness from the notes made concrete.
- **L9: the xkcd comic is CC BY-NC 2.5**, committed as a file (CI rejects external image
  URLs in decks) with a link back to xkcd.com/1838.
- **L9: scale the target.** An `MLPRegressor` on unscaled reactor pressure (about 2,705 kPa)
  predicts the mean; wrap it in `TransformedTargetRegressor`. The concrete network is left
  unscaled on purpose: its target is of order 10, and scaling it moved the grouped score to
  9.99 without a clean story.
- **L9: why not SysIdentPy for the NARX.** It would be one more dependency for one section, its
  headline idea (FROLS term selection for polynomial NARX) is a topic of its own, and its
  examples fit one experiment at a time, where TEP has hundreds of independent runs. It is in
  the notes' Resources.
- **L9: the trivia box** (violet, `:class: trivia` in the notes via `_static/course.css`, and
  `<div class="trivia">` in the deck via `themes/course.css`) is for facts outside the
  argument, such as the Hinton Nobel. Keep green for key terms.
- Students routinely report the *best validation* score as if it were test performance.
  Hammer the "test set touched once" rule and make them write the final number from a
  locked test split. The size of that optimism is measurable and is now measured: over a
  36-candidate grid it is **+0.19 MW at n=80, inside the fold noise by n≈320, and −0.003 MW
  on all 9,568 rows**. So nested CV is small-data insurance, not a universal tax, and the
  quantity that decides it is the ratio of candidates to rows. Do not let them conclude
  either "always nest" or "never bother."
- MLflow's local file store is enough; don't let students burn time standing up a tracking
  server. Do insist they log the dataset hash and git SHA — "reproducible" means someone
  else can regenerate the number.
- Optuna can overfit the validation set if the budget is huge. Cap trials, keep a locked
  test set, and discuss the 1-SE rule so "biggest model wins" doesn't become the reflex.
