# Week 06 — Deep Learning with PyTorch
**Arc:** Machine learning & deep learning · **Lectures:** L11–L12

**Prerequisites (from prior weeks):** The full ML workflow from Wk5 — baselines,
leakage-aware splits, cross-validation, metric selection — and fluency with MLflow tracking
(experiments, runs, autolog). Comfortable NumPy/array thinking and validated feature tables
from Wk3–Wk4. No prior PyTorch is assumed; this week builds it from tensors up.

## Module learning objectives
- Manipulate PyTorch **tensors** and understand automatic differentiation (`autograd`) well
  enough to explain what a backward pass computes and why gradients accumulate.
- Write a correct, from-scratch **training loop** (forward → loss → backward → step →
  zero-grad) with a `Dataset`/`DataLoader`, and move it to a **GPU** without silent errors.
- Choose an architecture that matches the *structure* of engineering data: **MLPs** for
  tabular/vector inputs, **CNNs** for fields and images, **1D-CNN / RNN / temporal models**
  for sensor time series.
- Regularize and stabilize training: normalization, dropout, weight decay, learning-rate
  schedules, early stopping, and reading loss curves to diagnose under/overfitting.
- Track deep-learning experiments in MLflow (per-epoch metrics, checkpoints, the model
  artifact) and reproduce a run from its logged config and seed.

## Lecture L11 — Tensors, autodiff, training loops, and GPUs
**Objectives:**
- Build fluency with tensors, shapes/broadcasting, and device placement.
- Explain and implement a training loop and the role of `autograd` and the optimizer.
- Use GPUs correctly and diagnose the common failure modes.

**Topics:**
- Tensors vs. NumPy arrays: dtypes, shapes, broadcasting, views vs. copies, `reshape`
  and the batch dimension. Why the batch dimension is first (`N, ...`).
- Automatic differentiation: the computation graph, `requires_grad`, `.backward()`,
  gradient accumulation and why you must `zero_grad()`, and `torch.no_grad()` for
  evaluation. A hand-derived one-layer example so the magic is demystified.
- The anatomy of a training loop: `Dataset` and `DataLoader` (batching, shuffling,
  `num_workers`), `nn.Module`, loss functions (`MSELoss`, `L1Loss`, `CrossEntropyLoss`),
  optimizers (SGD with momentum, Adam/AdamW), and the forward/backward/step/zero cycle.
- Devices and GPUs: `.to(device)`, keeping model and data on the same device, CPU↔GPU
  transfer cost, mixed precision (`torch.autocast`, `GradScaler`) at a glance, and the
  cloud+GPU workflow used for A6. Common bugs: device mismatch, forgetting `model.eval()`,
  exploding loss from a too-large learning rate, and NaNs from unnormalized inputs.
- Reproducibility in DL: seeding torch/NumPy/Python, deterministic dataloaders, and logging
  seeds to MLflow.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** Build a regression **MLP in PyTorch** for the UCI
**Concrete Compressive Strength** dataset (1,030 mixes: cement, blast-furnace slag, fly
ash, water, superplasticizer, coarse & fine aggregate, age → compressive strength, MPa).
Open with the gradient: derive it by hand for one hidden layer, then check `loss.backward()`,
`jax.grad`, and central finite differences against it. Autodiff matches the analytic answer to
float64 epsilon; the best finite difference is 1.4e-12 at h = 3e-7 and needs 2 evaluations per
parameter. Then write the loop by hand, plot the loss curve, and break it three times on
purpose. Finish with the tree comparison.

Two corrections to earlier drafts of this paragraph, both measured:

- **The GPU is not faster here, it is 2.5x slower** (8.6 ms/epoch on CPU against 21.1 on
  Apple MPS), and the crossover for a 3-layer MLP sits between 64 and 256 hidden units. Do
  *not* promise "the same results faster"; promise the same results, and let them watch the
  accelerator lose. The transferable lesson is the fixed cost per kernel launch, which every
  accelerator has, and the practical rule the teaching notes already give: debug on CPU.
- **The tree does not simply win.** Under a random k-fold gradient boosting beats the MLP by
  0.41 +/- 0.09 MPa. Under a `GroupKFold` on the mix the gap is 0.09 +/- 0.10, a tie. See the
  grouping note below; the honest comparison is the interesting one, not the leaky one.

**Engineering framing / dataset:** Concrete strength is a classic engineering surrogate:
predict an expensive 28-day destructive test from mix proportions. It is small and tabular
on purpose: it makes the honest point that deep nets do **not** automatically win on
tabular engineering data, motivating the architecture-matching discussion in L12.

**The dataset is grouped, and nothing in the file says so.** Group the rows by the seven mix
components, ignoring age, and 1,030 rows collapse into **428 distinct mixes**; 182 of those
were tested at more than one age, covering **76% of all rows**. The same batch of concrete
appears at 3, 7, 28 and 90 days as separate rows, so a random k-fold asks the model to fill in
a curing curve it has already seen most of. There are also **25 exact duplicate rows**. This
makes the dataset a second worked example of Wk5's `GroupKFold` lesson, on a different material,
and it is the reason the DL-vs-tree comparison has to be run twice to mean anything.

A rough noise floor is available too: eight settings have the same mix at the same age measured
twice, giving a repeatability of about **2.2 MPa on 8 degrees of freedom**. Quote it as an order
of magnitude, not a number. (One four-row group is excluded: three copies of 55.895819 MPa and
one 22.897498, which is a bad number rather than scatter.)

## Lecture L12 — Architectures for engineering data: MLP, CNN, and sequence models
**Objectives:**
- Match model architecture to the structure of the input (vector, field/image, sequence).
- Implement a 1D-CNN or RNN for sensor time series and a 2D-CNN for field/image data.
- Apply regularization and normalization appropriate to each architecture.

**Topics:**
- MLPs for tabular/vector data: width vs. depth, activations (ReLU/GELU), batch/layer
  normalization, dropout, and why feature scaling still matters for gradient descent.
- CNNs for spatial fields and images: convolution, kernels, channels, stride, padding,
  pooling, receptive field, and parameter sharing. Engineering fields as "images" —
  temperature/stress/velocity maps from simulation. Preview of encoder–decoder / U-Net
  shapes used for field-to-field surrogates (developed further in Wk7).
- Sequence models for sensor time series: 1D-CNN over time, RNN/LSTM/GRU for long-range
  dependence, and windowing/normalization of multivariate sensor streams. When a temporal
  CNN beats an RNN (short windows, cheaper training) and when attention/Transformers help.
- Training deep nets well: learning-rate schedules (step, cosine, one-cycle), early
  stopping on validation loss, gradient clipping, weight decay, and checkpointing the best
  model. Reading loss curves to tell underfit from overfit from bad optimization.
- MLflow for DL: log per-epoch train/val loss, log the best checkpoint as an artifact, and
  register the final model.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** Frame **NASA C-MAPSS turbofan** remaining-useful-life
(RUL) prediction (FD001 subset: 21 sensor channels + 3 operating settings over run-to-
failure engine cycles). Build a **1D-CNN** over sliding sensor windows to regress RUL; use
**GroupKFold by engine unit** so no engine leaks across splits; log training to MLflow and
compare against a Wk5 tabular baseline on hand-crafted window features. Discuss the
piecewise-linear RUL target convention and why grouping by engine is non-negotiable.

**Engineering framing / dataset:** Turbofan RUL is the reference **prognostics /
predictive-maintenance** problem — noisy multivariate sensors, unit-to-unit variation, and
a target you only observe at failure. It directly foreshadows the miniproject: a surrogate/
predictive model on real engineering sensor data with proper grouped evaluation. Alternate
sequence dataset for demo/office hours: **CWRU bearing vibration** signals (fault
classification from accelerometer time series).

## Teaching notes / common pitfalls
- The number-one first-day bug is device mismatch and `zero_grad()` omission. Have students
  write the loop by hand once before showing any high-level trainer, so they can debug it.
  Measured cost of the missing `zero_grad()` on concrete: **17.8 MPa validation RMSE against
  6.0**, where predicting the training mean scores 19.2, so the broken run keeps about 11% of
  what the working one earned. It oscillates rather than diverging, which is why it reads as a
  tuning problem rather than a bug.
- Budget time for the **dtype trap**: `torch.tensor(3.14)` is float32 while
  `torch.tensor(np.float64(3.14))` is float64, and because float32 promotes on contact, every
  dtype downstream of the mistake reports float64. Two stray Python floats in the autodiff
  example cost a relative error of 1.25e-7, which is float32 epsilon, and briefly looked like a
  real disagreement between PyTorch and JAX.
- Manage expectations, but state the result precisely, because it is more interesting than
  the slogan. On concrete, over 5 seeds x 5 folds: under a random k-fold the tree beats the MLP
  by 0.41 +/- 0.09 MPa; under a mix-grouped split the gap is 0.09 +/- 0.10 MPa, smaller than its
  own standard error. Both models improve under the leak, but the **tree improves more** (1.27
  MPa against the MLP's 0.94), so a good part of "trees beat nets on tabular data" is here a
  statement about the split. That is still a *feature* of the lesson: architecture must match
  data structure and size, DL is not a default, and a model-family conclusion drawn on a leaky
  split can evaporate when the leak is closed. Do not overclaim in the other direction either:
  Grinsztajn et al. benchmarked 45 datasets and trees remain state of the art.
- **Insist on multiple seeds.** A single-seed version of the same comparison showed the MLP
  *winning* under the grouped split; five seeds show a tie. Seed spread on this problem is as
  large as the model-family difference, so a one-run comparison is not evidence.
- For turbofan/bearings, group leakage (windows from the same engine/bearing in both train
  and validation) silently inflates scores. Make GroupKFold-by-unit a graded requirement.
- Unnormalized inputs cause NaNs and dead training **with SGD**, and something worse with
  Adam: measured on concrete, SGD on raw inputs gives `nan` inside the first epoch, while Adam
  quietly trains to 9.7 MPa instead of 6.0 and reports nothing wrong. Adam's per-parameter step
  size absorbs a scaling bug and hands back mediocrity. Fold scaling into the pipeline and fit
  it on training data only, exactly as in Wk4-Wk5, and tell them which failure each optimizer
  produces.
- Learning-rate blow-up is worth demonstrating at **lr = 2** with plain SGD, which gives `nan`
  from the first epoch every time. lr = 1 sits on the stability boundary: across six
  seed-and-loop combinations it produced `nan` in three and diverged to 90-170 MPa in the
  others. That seed dependence is itself the point.
- GPU queue time is real. Tell students to debug on a tiny subset / few epochs on CPU, then
  launch the full run on GPU — and to log everything to MLflow so a crashed cloud instance
  doesn't lose the experiment.
- Watch for silent test-set peeking during early-stopping tuning; early stopping uses the
  *validation* split, and the test split is still touched once.
