# Week 06 — ML with PyTorch/JAX, and architectures for engineering data
**Arc:** Machine learning & deep learning · **Lectures:** L11–L12

**Realigned 2026-09-22** with the schedule (L11 "ML with PyTorch/JAX", L12 "ML architectures
for engineering data") and with what L9 now teaches. The L12 block below is a **proposal**,

**Prerequisites (from prior weeks):** L9: the four scikit-learn families (linear, tree, MLP,
GP) on concrete and TEP, **training written as an optimization problem** (least squares; a
non-convex problem over weights and biases, with ten random starts; the GP's marginal
likelihood), the mix-grouped concrete split (428 mixes, `GroupKFold`), capacity, validation
and learning curves, and NN-NARX/GP-NARX on L8's pressure table, where the GP stopped at a few
thousand rows. L10: tree ensembles and thresholds (as proposed in wk05), hyperparameter search,
and MLflow tracking. No prior PyTorch is assumed; this week builds it from tensors up.

**The through-line.** L9 wrote training as $\min_\theta L(\theta)$ and let scikit-learn solve
it. L11 solves it by hand: autograd computes the gradient the optimizer used, the training loop
is that optimizer written out, and mini-batches are how it scales past the wall L9 hit. L12
asks what $f(x;\theta)$ should look like when the data has a shape.

## Module learning objectives
- Manipulate PyTorch **tensors** and understand automatic differentiation (`autograd`) well
  enough to explain what a backward pass computes and why gradients accumulate.
- Write a correct, from-scratch **training loop** (forward → loss → backward → step →
  zero-grad) with a `Dataset`/`DataLoader`, and move it to a **GPU** without silent errors.
- Choose an architecture that matches the *structure* of engineering data (its inductive
  bias): **MLPs** for tables, **1D-CNN / RNN** for sensor time series, **CNNs** for fields on a
  grid, and **message passing** for graphs.
- Regularize and stabilize training: normalization, dropout, weight decay, learning-rate
  schedules, early stopping, and reading loss curves to diagnose under/overfitting.
- Track deep-learning experiments in MLflow (per-epoch metrics, checkpoints, the model
  artifact) and reproduce a run from its logged config and seed.

## Lecture L11 — ML with PyTorch/JAX: tensors, autodiff, training loops, and GPUs
**Framing (2026-09-22).** Open on L9's training problem, $\min_{W,b} \sum_i (y_i -
f(x_i;W,b))^2$, and on the two places L9 stopped: `MLPRegressor.fit` hid the optimizer, and the
GP-NARX could not use more than a few thousand of 144,300 rows. Autograd is how the gradient in
that problem gets computed, the loop is the optimizer written out, and a mini-batch is a noisy
gradient from a slice of the data, which is what lets training scale. `jax.grad` is the same
derivative as a function transformation, and `jit`/`vmap` are the JAX half of the title.

**Objectives:**
- Build fluency with tensors, shapes/broadcasting, and device placement.
- Explain and implement a training loop and the role of `autograd` and the optimizer.
- Use GPUs correctly and diagnose the common failure modes.

**Topics:**
- Tensors vs. NumPy arrays: dtypes, shapes, broadcasting, views vs. copies, `reshape`
  and the batch dimension. Why the batch dimension is first (`N, ...`).
- Automatic differentiation: the computation graph, `requires_grad`, `.backward()`,
  gradient accumulation and why you must `zero_grad()`, and `torch.no_grad()` for
  evaluation. A hand-derived one-layer example so the magic is demystified.
- The anatomy of a training loop: `Dataset` and `DataLoader` (batching, shuffling,
  `num_workers`), `nn.Module`, loss functions (`MSELoss`, `L1Loss`, `CrossEntropyLoss`),
  optimizers (SGD with momentum, Adam/AdamW), and the forward/backward/step/zero cycle.
- Devices and GPUs: `.to(device)`, keeping model and data on the same device, CPU↔GPU
  transfer cost, mixed precision (`torch.autocast`, `GradScaler`) at a glance, and the
  cloud+GPU workflow used for A6. Common bugs: device mismatch, forgetting `model.eval()`,
  exploding loss from a too-large learning rate, and NaNs from unnormalized inputs.
- Reproducibility in DL: seeding torch/NumPy/Python, deterministic dataloaders, and logging
  seeds to MLflow.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** Build a regression **MLP in PyTorch** for the UCI
**Concrete Compressive Strength** dataset (1,030 mixes: cement, blast-furnace slag, fly
ash, water, superplasticizer, coarse & fine aggregate, age → compressive strength, MPa).
Open with the gradient: derive it by hand for one hidden layer, then check `loss.backward()`,
`jax.grad`, and central finite differences against it. Autodiff matches the analytic answer to
float64 epsilon; the best finite difference is 1.4e-12 at h = 3e-7 and needs 2 evaluations per
parameter. Then write the loop by hand, plot the loss curve, and break it three times on
purpose. Finish with the tree comparison. The concrete MLP in scikit-learn scored 8.02 MPa under
L9's `GroupKFold`; say so, and show the hand-written loop reaching the same neighbourhood, so
students see the same model with the machinery exposed.

Two corrections to earlier drafts of this paragraph, both measured:

- **The GPU is not faster here, it is 2.5x slower** (8.6 ms/epoch on CPU against 21.1 on
  Apple MPS), and the crossover for a 3-layer MLP sits between 64 and 256 hidden units. Do
  *not* promise "the same results faster"; promise the same results, and let them watch the
  accelerator lose. The transferable lesson is the fixed cost per kernel launch, which every
  accelerator has, and the practical rule the teaching notes already give: debug on CPU.
- **The tree does not simply win.** Under a random k-fold gradient boosting beats the MLP by
  0.41 +/- 0.09 MPa. Under a `GroupKFold` on the mix the gap is 0.09 +/- 0.10, a tie. See the
  grouping note below; the honest comparison is the interesting one, not the leaky one.

**Engineering framing / dataset:** Concrete strength is a classic engineering surrogate:
predict an expensive 28-day destructive test from mix proportions. It is small and tabular
on purpose: it makes the honest point that deep nets do **not** automatically win on
tabular engineering data, motivating the architecture-matching discussion in L12.

**The grouping is L9's, not new here.** L9 introduced concrete and its structure (1,030 rows,
**428 distinct mixes**, 182 multi-age mixes holding **76%** of the rows, **25 exact
duplicates**) and used `GroupKFold` on the mix throughout. L11 cites it and uses the grouped
split from the start, rather than rediscovering it; the random-versus-grouped comparison below
is still worth one slide, because it changes the net-versus-tree verdict.

A rough noise floor is available too: eight settings have the same mix at the same age measured
twice, giving a repeatability of about **2.2 MPa on 8 degrees of freedom**. Quote it as an order
of magnitude, not a number. (One four-row group is excluded: three copies of 55.895819 MPa and
one 22.897498, which is a bad number rather than scatter.)

**Readings & resources:**

  and "Datasets & DataLoaders."
- Bourke, "Learn PyTorch for Deep Learning" (learnpytorch.io) — sections 00–03.
- Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux, "Why do tree-based models still outperform deep learning

- I-Cheng Yeh, "Modeling of strength of high-performance concrete using artificial neural

## Lecture L12 — ML architectures for engineering data (proposal, 2026-09-22)
**The session in one sentence:** an architecture is an assumption about the shape of the data
(its inductive bias), and the right assumption is data you do not have to collect.

**Why this framing.** By L12 students have seen an MLP fed a flat vector twice: the NN-NARX in
L9, which tied a linear ARX, and the concrete MLP in L11, which tied gradient boosting. An MLP
knows nothing about order in time, neighbours on a grid, or bonds in a molecule, so every such
structure has to be learned from data. L12 is about building it in instead. It also finishes the
ladder L7 started (ARX → NARX → state space → RNN) and gives the PSE students two hooks they
already own: a recurrent network is a discrete-time state-space model, and a convolution is a
finite-difference stencil.

**What not to do here, because other sessions own it:** field-to-field surrogates, U-Nets,
operator learning and PINNs are L13's; transformers and attention are L15's (name attention in
one line only); hyperparameter search is L10's (L12 only uses early stopping and a learning-rate
schedule).

**Objectives:**
- Explain inductive bias, and match an architecture to the structure of the input: an MLP for
  a table, a 1D-CNN or RNN for a sensor time series, a CNN for a field on a grid, message
  passing for a graph.
- Relate a 1D-CNN and an RNN to the NARX and state-space models of L7, and choose between them
  for a given sensor problem.
- Implement a 1D-CNN and a GRU in PyTorch for sensor windows, and compare them with an MLP on
  the flattened window and with a tree ensemble, on a split grouped by unit.
- Explain a 2D convolution as a finite-difference stencil, and message passing as its
  generalization to an irregular mesh or graph.

**Topics** (about 60% sequences, 20% fields, 10% graphs, 10% training and limitations):
- **Inductive bias and the shapes of engineering data.** One table: data shape → structure the
  model should know → architecture → example (a table of experiments → nothing beyond the
  columns → MLP or trees → concrete; a sensor series → order, local patterns, a hidden state →
  1D-CNN, RNN → C-MAPSS, TEP; a field on a grid → locality, the same physics everywhere → CNN →
  a temperature field from a solver; a graph → neighbours, no natural order → message passing →
  molecules, flowsheets, pipe networks).
- **Sequences, as the end of L7's ladder.** ARX (linear, fixed lags) → NARX-MLP (nonlinear,
  each lag its own weight, fixed window) → **1D-CNN** (one small filter slid along time:
  weight sharing, local patterns, a receptive field that grows with depth and dilation) →
  **RNN** ($h_{t+1} = \sigma(A h_t + B u_t)$, $y_t = C h_t$: L7's discrete-time state-space
  form with a learned nonlinear state) → why gradients vanish or explode through time → the
  gates of **LSTM/GRU**. A temporal CNN for fixed windows, a recurrent model for long or
  variable-length dependence; attention named in one line as "every step looks at every other".
- **Fields: a convolution is a stencil.** The 5-point finite-difference Laplacian is a 3 × 3
  kernel, so `conv2d` with that kernel reproduces the FD Laplacian of a field to rounding (show
  it in three lines, then let the kernel be learned). Channels as fields (T, u, v), stride and
  pooling as coarser grids (the multigrid picture), the receptive field. Concept and a small
  classifier or regressor only; training a field-to-field surrogate is L13.
- **Graphs, at a glance.** Message passing, $h_i \leftarrow \phi\big(h_i, \sum_{j \in N(i)}
  \psi(h_i, h_j)\big)$: a convolution on an irregular mesh. Molecules (atoms and bonds; Gilmer et
  al. 2017 for quantum chemistry), flowsheets (units and streams), sensor and pipe networks.
  No demo; one figure and the equation.
- **Training these well, briefly.** Normalization per channel fitted on training units, early
  stopping on validation loss, a learning-rate schedule, gradient clipping (which RNNs need),
  checkpointing the best model to MLflow. Reading loss curves: underfit, overfit, or bad
  optimization.
- **Limitations.** A matched prior can be wrong (a CNN assumes the same physics everywhere; a
  boundary condition breaks that). Depth needs data, and a hundred engines is small. On small
  tabular data trees still win (Grinsztajn et al. 2022).

**Live demo / in-class activity:** **NASA C-MAPSS FD001** turbofan remaining useful life,
introduced here (it is no longer used in L7 or L8): 100 training engines run to failure, 30-cycle
windows over the varying sensor and setting channels, the piecewise-linear RUL target capped at
125 cycles, and `GroupKFold` by engine. Four models on the same windows: gradient boosting on
window summary features, an MLP on the flattened window (the NARX way), a 1D-CNN, and a GRU.
The existing draft measured the first and third: **1D-CNN 19.9 ± 2.0 cycles against boosting
18.3 ± 1.0**, a tie inside the CNN's fold spread; the MLP and GRU need measuring, with several
seeds. Plus the three-line stencil demonstration. TEP stays out of L12 on purpose: L12 is on
10-05, the miniproject is due 10-09, and a GRU forecaster on TEP is the miniproject's stretch
goal.

**Engineering framing / dataset:** Turbofan RUL is the reference **prognostics /
predictive-maintenance** problem: noisy multivariate sensors, unit-to-unit variation, and a
target you only observe at failure. Grouping by engine is non-negotiable, for the same reason
L9 grouped concrete by mix and L8 split TEP by run.

**Alternatives considered:** (1) the same spine with a TEP demo (most continuity; rejected for
the miniproject overlap above); (2) sequences only, one deeper session on 1D-CNN/RNN/LSTM
(drops fields and graphs; stronger if L13 also wants to own the convolution idea).

## Teaching notes / common pitfalls
- The number-one first-day bug is device mismatch and `zero_grad()` omission. Have students
  write the loop by hand once before showing any high-level trainer, so they can debug it.
  Measured cost of the missing `zero_grad()` on concrete: **17.8 MPa validation RMSE against
  6.0**, where predicting the training mean scores 19.2, so the broken run keeps about 11% of
  what the working one earned. It oscillates rather than diverging, which is why it reads as a
  tuning problem rather than a bug.
- Budget time for the **dtype trap**: `torch.tensor(3.14)` is float32 while
  `torch.tensor(np.float64(3.14))` is float64, and because float32 promotes on contact, every
  dtype downstream of the mistake reports float64. Two stray Python floats in the autodiff
  example cost a relative error of 1.25e-7, which is float32 epsilon, and briefly looked like a
  real disagreement between PyTorch and JAX.
- Manage expectations, but state the result precisely, because it is more interesting than
  the slogan. On concrete, over 5 seeds x 5 folds: under a random k-fold the tree beats the MLP
  by 0.41 +/- 0.09 MPa; under a mix-grouped split the gap is 0.09 +/- 0.10 MPa, smaller than its
  own standard error. Both models improve under the leak, but the **tree improves more** (1.27
  MPa against the MLP's 0.94), so a good part of "trees beat nets on tabular data" is here a
  statement about the split. That is still a *feature* of the lesson: architecture must match
  data structure and size, DL is not a default, and a model-family conclusion drawn on a leaky
  split can evaporate when the leak is closed. Do not overclaim in the other direction either:
  Grinsztajn et al. benchmarked 45 datasets and trees remain state of the art.
- **Insist on multiple seeds.** A single-seed version of the same comparison showed the MLP
  *winning* under the grouped split; five seeds show a tie. Seed spread on this problem is as
  large as the model-family difference, so a one-run comparison is not evidence.
- For turbofan/bearings, group leakage (windows from the same engine/bearing in both train
  and validation) silently inflates scores. Make GroupKFold-by-unit a graded requirement.
- **Do not re-teach L9 in L11.** The concrete grouping, the four families, and "training is an
  optimization problem" are L9's. L11's job is to make the optimizer visible, not to reintroduce
  the model.
- **L12's GRU needs gradient clipping**; without it an exploding gradient through time looks like
  a learning-rate problem. Measure it before promising it.
- Unnormalized inputs cause NaNs and dead training **with SGD**, and something worse with
  Adam: measured on concrete, SGD on raw inputs gives `nan` inside the first epoch, while Adam
  quietly trains to 9.7 MPa instead of 6.0 and reports nothing wrong. Adam's per-parameter step
  size absorbs a scaling bug and hands back mediocrity. Fold scaling into the pipeline and fit
  it on training data only, exactly as in Wk4-Wk5, and tell them which failure each optimizer
  produces.
- Learning-rate blow-up is worth demonstrating at **lr = 2** with plain SGD, which gives `nan`
  from the first epoch every time. lr = 1 sits on the stability boundary: across six
  seed-and-loop combinations it produced `nan` in three and diverged to 90-170 MPa in the
  others. That seed dependence is itself the point.
- GPU queue time is real. Tell students to debug on a tiny subset / few epochs on CPU, then
  launch the full run on GPU — and to log everything to MLflow so a crashed cloud instance
  doesn't lose the experiment.
- Watch for silent test-set peeking during early-stopping tuning; early stopping uses the
  *validation* split, and the test split is still touched once.
