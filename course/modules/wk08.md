## Module learning objectives
- Frame "which experiment or simulation next?" as a sequential decision under uncertainty.
- Run a Bayesian-optimization loop, compute and compare the common acquisition functions, and
  use BoTorch.
- Contrast active learning with Bayesian optimization: the same loop, with the goal of a model
  that is good everywhere rather than one optimum.
- Report a design campaign honestly: several seeds, a random baseline, and the evaluation
  budget counted.
- Explain what a generative model learns, fit one to engineering data, and sample it
  conditionally to answer an inverse question that has more than one answer.

## Lecture L15 — Bayesian optimization
**Changed 2026-10-20:** L15 is Bayesian optimization only. L14 covered uncertainty quantification
(Gaussian processes, calibration, ensembles, conformal prediction), so the UQ sections, the
calibration widget and the concrete strength example leave L15. Active learning shrinks to one
closing slide. The deck has no clicker questions. All code is BoTorch.

**Objectives:**
- Explain what Bayesian optimization (BO) is, why it is called Bayesian, and when it beats
  gradient-based and other derivative-free optimizers: expensive, noisy, black-box objectives
  with a budget of tens of evaluations.
- Explain the BO loop (a Gaussian-process surrogate, an acquisition function, evaluate, update)
  and the trade-off between exploration and exploitation.
- Write and compute the common acquisition functions: probability of improvement (PI), expected
  improvement (EI), upper and lower confidence bounds (UCB, LCB) and Thompson sampling, and
  explain how their parameters set the balance between exploration and exploitation.
- Run BO in BoTorch on an engineering problem and compare it with random search over several
  seeds.
- Name the extensions engineers need (constraints, batches, several objectives, several
  fidelities) and recognize where BO is used across engineering and science.

**Topics:**
- **The problem.** Every evaluation is an experiment or a simulation; the budget is tens of
  evaluations; there are no gradients; the measurements are noisy. Grid and random search waste
  that budget.
- **Gen 0 to Gen 1** (L13 and the CMU seminar): a surrogate that only predicts (Gen 0) against
  one whose uncertainty chooses the next experiment, "machine learning with intention" (Gen 1).
  BO is Gen 1.
- **Why "Bayesian".** A prior over functions, data, Bayes' rule, a posterior; decisions taken
  on the posterior. The GP is recapped from L14 in two slides, not retaught.
- **Definitions** (green boxes): black-box objective, surrogate, acquisition function,
  incumbent (best so far), budget, regret.
- **The loop:** fit the GP, maximize the acquisition function, evaluate, update.
- **Exploration and exploitation:** definitions, the two extremes (pure exploitation gets stuck
  in a local optimum; pure exploration is random search), and the knobs ($\kappa$, $\xi$).
- **Acquisition functions**, each equation annotated term by term, with
  $z = (\mu(x) - f^* - \xi)/\sigma(x)$:
  - PI: $\Phi(z)$ (Kushner, 1964).
  - EI: $(\mu - f^* - \xi)\,\Phi(z) + \sigma\,\phi(z)$, derived from its definition (Mockus;
    Jones, Schonlau and Welch, 1998).
  - UCB $\mu + \kappa\sigma$ for maximizing, LCB $\mu - \kappa\sigma$ for minimizing (GP-UCB,
    Srinivas et al., 2010).
  - Thompson sampling: one draw from the posterior, evaluated at its optimum.
  - LogEI: the numerically safe form of EI (Ament et al., 2023).
  - One GP, four acquisitions, four different next points.
- **History:** Kushner (1964), Mockus (1970s, the "Bayesian" approach to global optimization),
  Jones, Schonlau and Welch (1998, EGO, which made EI with a GP the standard), Jones (2001, a
  taxonomy of response-surface methods), Snoek et al. (2012, BO for machine learning).
- **BoTorch:** what it is (PyTorch, GPyTorch, Monte Carlo acquisition functions), docs and
  tutorials, then the code of the worked example on annotated slides, identical to the
  notebook: normalize the inputs and standardize the outputs, `SingleTaskGP`,
  `fit_gpytorch_mll`, `LogExpectedImprovement` and `UpperConfidenceBound`, `optimize_acqf`,
  the loop.
- **Honest reporting:** several seeds, a random-search baseline, best-so-far against
  evaluations with its spread, the budget counted.
- **BO against gradient-based optimization:** a decision map (cost per evaluation, gradients
  available, noise, dimension, budget) and a measured comparison of evaluations to reach a
  target for BO, L-BFGS with finite differences, Nelder–Mead and random search.
- **Applications**, each with its source, verified before use: reaction optimization against
  expert chemists (Shields et al., 2021); a mobile robotic chemist (Burger et al., 2020); battery
  fast-charging protocols (Attia et al., 2020); flowsheet and process design with kriging
  surrogates (Caballero and Grossmann, 2008); engineering design (Forrester, Sóbester and Keane,
  2008); machine-learning hyperparameters (Snoek et al., 2012) and AlphaGo (Chen et al., 2018);
  controller tuning; A/B testing with noisy experiments (Letham et al., 2019).
- **Extensions**, one slide: constraints, batches (qEI), several objectives (qEHVI), several
  fidelities; all in BoTorch.
- **Limitations and trade-offs:** dimension (BO works best below about 20 inputs), the $O(n^3)$
  cost of the GP, kernel choice, discontinuities.
- **Active learning**, one closing slide: the same loop with a different goal, a model that is
  good everywhere instead of one optimum.

**Animations** (interactive widgets, Play/Replay or a slider, built from `make_figures.py`):
- The GP posterior updating as points arrive: why it is Bayesian.
- The BO loop step by step: GP, acquisition, next point, evaluation, best-so-far.
- An exploration–exploitation slider ($\kappa$ in UCB, $\xi$ in EI) moving the next pick.
- BO, L-BFGS, Nelder–Mead and random search racing on the same objective, with evaluation
  counters.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** BoTorch on the reactor. First one input (temperature),
watching EI, UCB and Thompson sampling choose different points as the GP updates; then two
inputs (temperature and residence time), BO against random search over five seeds, best-so-far
with its spread.

**Engineering framing / dataset:** series reactions A → B → C in a continuous stirred tank. The
product is the intermediate B. Too cold and A barely reacts; too hot and B goes on to C. Choose
the temperature (then the temperature and the residence time) that maximizes the yield of B.
Arrhenius kinetics with the second step's activation energy above the first, so the optimum is
interior. Each evaluation is one steady-state experiment, with measurement noise. The model is
known in closed form, so every number in the lecture can be checked against the true optimum.

**Deck:** about 60 slides, no clicker questions, every code block copied from the worked
example, keyword arguments one per line.

## Lecture L16 — Generative AI: learning a distribution and sampling from it
**Changed 2026-10-11:** replaces active learning and reinforcement learning (see the week
header). The lecture runs about 70 minutes, which leaves the rest of the session for the
notebook and questions. The anchor paper is Alves & Kitchin, "Generative machine learning
approaches to optimization" (ChemRxiv, 2026, doi:10.26434/chemrxiv-2025-hk886/v3), whose
examples are chemical-engineering problems and whose code is public.

**Objectives:**
- Distinguish a generative model from the regression and classification models of L8–L14, and
  explain why a regression model gives a wrong answer to an inverse question with more than
  one solution.
- Fit a Gaussian mixture model to the joint distribution of inputs and outputs, choose the
  number of components, and condition it to sample the inputs that give a target output.
- Explain how flow matching turns samples of a simple distribution into samples of the data,
  train one with a mean-squared-error loss, and sample it by integrating an ODE.
- Pose root finding, optimization and parameter estimation as conditional sampling, and check
  the samples against the physical model.
- Place generative models in the machine-learning landscape, and explain why they are as
  dependent on data, and as limited by it, as any regression model.
- Describe how generative models are used for images, molecules, proteins, materials and
  weather, and the failure modes those uses share.

**Topics** (minutes are a budget for the 70-minute lecture):
- **The inverse question regression gets wrong** (5 min). Data from $y = |x|$: ask which $x$
  gives $y = 0.75$. A regression of $x$ on $y$ returns about 0, the average of the two answers
  $\pm 0.75$, and that average is not a solution at all. The same thing in engineering: the
  Peng–Robinson cubic for propane at 300 K and 10 bar has three roots (liquid, unstable,
  vapor), and Newton's method returns whichever one the initial guess is near.
- **What a generative model is** (10 min). The data are samples from an unknown distribution
  $p(x)$; a generative model learns it well enough to draw new samples, and sometimes to
  evaluate the density. An image is a vector of pixel values, so image generation is sampling
  a distribution over very long vectors; a row of (inputs, outputs, derivatives) is the same
  kind of vector. Joint, marginal and conditional distributions; conditioning as "fix some
  coordinates, sample the rest". Discriminative models (L8–L14) learn $p(y \mid x)$ or only
  $E[y \mid x]$; a generative model of the joint can answer in either direction, or a mixed
  one. Two ways to make $p(x)$ learnable, which organize the rest of the lecture: write down a
  density family and fit it (explicit joint), or learn a map from a distribution that is easy
  to sample to the data (transformation). A third way, factoring the joint with the chain rule
  $p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_{<i})$, is named here and is the family language
  models belong to.
- **Approach 1: learn the joint, then condition it. Gaussian mixture models** (15 min).
  The mixture density $p(x, y) = \sum_k \pi_k \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$; fitting by
  expectation maximization, in words (soft assignment, then weighted means and covariances);
  choosing $K$ with BIC. Conditioning one Gaussian has a closed form (the Schur complement:
  conditional mean $\mu_y + \Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}(x - \mu_x)$, conditional covariance
  $\Sigma_{yy} - \Sigma_{yx}\Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\Sigma_{xy}$), and conditioning the mixture
  reweights the components by how well each explains the observed coordinates. The worked
  cases from Alves & Kitchin, in increasing order of cleverness:
  - root finding: learn $p(Z, r(Z))$ for the Peng–Robinson residual and condition on $r = 0$;
    all three roots come out of one sample set (paper Table 1: 0.035, 0.128, 0.814 against
    0.0348, 0.1280, 0.8146);
  - unconstrained optimization: learn $p(\tau, C_B, dC_B/d\tau)$ for $A \to B \to C$ in a batch
    reactor and condition on the derivative being zero ($\tau^* \approx 3.05$ min);
  - parameter estimation: learn $p(k_1, k_2, F_A, F_B)$ for a plug-flow reactor and condition
    on the observed exit flows, which returns a distribution of rate constants instead of a
    single fit;
  - constraints, in one slide: condition on the gradient of the Lagrangian being zero
    (gasoline blending), or on a barrier-augmented objective for inequalities.
  Where GMMs stop: a full covariance has $D(D+1)/2$ entries per component and EM costs
  $O(D^3)$, so they work for tens of variables, not for an image.
- **Approach 2: transform a simple distribution** (20 min).
  - The simplest transformation students already know: inverse-CDF sampling turns uniform
    numbers into any 1-D distribution. Change of variables, and why a learned map must keep
    track of how it stretches volume.
  - Normalizing flows: a stack of invertible layers, exact likelihood, and the cost of the
    Jacobian determinant that restricts the architecture.
  - Continuous flows: let the map be the solution of an ODE $dx/dt = v_\theta(x, t)$, which is
    L13's neural ODE used to move a whole distribution.
  - Diffusion, in one picture: add noise until the data is Gaussian, learn to undo it step by
    step (DDPM, score-based SDEs). The idea behind most image generators since 2022.
  - **Flow matching**: pick a noise sample $x_0$ and a data sample $x_1$, form
    $x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1$, and train $v_\theta(x_t, t)$ by mean squared error to the
    velocity $x_1 - x_0$. Training is regression, which students have done since L9. Sampling
    is Euler integration of the ODE from $t = 0$ to 1 (100 steps in the paper). Conditioning
    is an extra input to the network, $v_\theta(x_t, c, t)$.
  - The same two worked cases as the GMM ($y = |x|$ and the Peng–Robinson roots), so the two
    approaches are compared on identical problems.
- **Where generative models fit in machine learning** (added 2026-10-11, folded into the
  5 minutes below with the comparison table). Supervised, unsupervised, conditional and
  self-supervised; next to Bayesian inversion. Still data-driven: a generative model is a
  surrogate of the data, needs more data than a regression for the same job, is valid only
  where the data are, inherits the data's flaws (a historian model learns the plant as
  operated), answers for the surrogate, and still has hyperparameters. A pros-and-cons table
  against a solver on a model and a discriminative surrogate.
- **Choosing between them** (5 min). A contrast table: what is learned (density or velocity
  field), training (EM, seconds; network, minutes), sampling (exact draw; ODE solve with many
  network calls), conditioning (any subset of variables after training for a GMM; only the
  variables fed to the network at training time for conditional flow matching), dimension
  (tens against millions), and density evaluation (exact for GMMs and flows, not directly
  available from flow matching). Generative optimization does not replace a solver; it finds
  every solution of a surrogate model, and a conventional solver or the physical model should
  check them.
- **Survey of generative models in science and engineering** (10 min). One slide per domain,
  each with a primary source and the check applied to the generated samples:
  - images: GANs, then latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion), then rectified-flow transformers
    (Stable Diffusion 3), which is flow matching at scale;
  - molecules: a VAE latent space for molecular design (Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018),
    equivariant diffusion in 3-D, diffusion for docking (DiffDock);
  - proteins: RFdiffusion, with designs made and measured in the lab, and the diffusion module
    in AlphaFold 3;
  - materials: CDVAE and MatterGen, with a generated material synthesized and measured;
  - weather: GenCast, a diffusion model producing ensemble forecasts, compared with ECMWF's
    operational ensemble;
  - inverse problems and optimization in process engineering: invertible networks (Ardizzone
    et al., 2019) and the anchor paper.
  The pattern to name: every one of these generates candidates and then checks them with a
  physics model, a simulation or an experiment.
- **Limitations and trade-offs** (5 min). The samples are solutions of the surrogate, not of
  the real system; conditioning outside the training data returns confident nonsense; sharp
  features the data do not resolve are missed; conditional flow matching fixes the
  conditioning variables at training time; generated molecules and structures can be invalid;
  models can memorize and reproduce training data (Carlini et al., 2023); training on
  generated data degrades a model (Shumailov et al., 2024); and there is no single metric for
  "the samples are right", so evaluation has to be designed per problem.

**Live demo / in-class activity:** `l16-generative-optimization.ipynb`, adapted from the
anchor paper's public repository (github.com/KitchinHUB/generative-optimization), CPU only
and seeded. (1) $y = |x|$ with the data around zero removed: a regression of $x$ on $y$
fails, a scikit-learn `GaussianMixture` with the conditioning written out in about twenty

**Engineering framing / dataset:** Synthetic data generated from engineering models the
students can check exactly: the Peng–Robinson equation of state for propane at 300 K and
10 bar, consecutive reactions $A \to B \to C$ in a batch reactor and in a plug-flow reactor.
Each has a known answer, so every sample can be checked against the true model.

## Teaching notes / common pitfalls
- BO is stochastic, so a single lucky run is not evidence. Require several seeds and a
  random-search baseline before anyone claims BO "worked".
- Keep the budget honest: the surrogate exists to avoid expensive evaluations, so students
  count and report how many they used.
- Library: BoTorch only in L15. Students find scikit-optimize first in search results; it is
  archived.
- Sign errors: UCB for maximizing, LCB for minimizing, and BoTorch maximizes by default. Most
  "BO does not work" bugs are an objective with the wrong sign.
- Plain EI goes flat far from the data and its gradients vanish; use LogEI in BoTorch.
- BO has a cold start: with two or three points the GP's uncertainty is not trustworthy yet,
  so seed with a small space-filling design before the loop.
- GMM conditioning is only as good as the fit: with too few components the conditional
  smears across both roots; with too many it overfits noise. Show BIC against $K$ before
  conditioning anything.
- Standardize every column before fitting either model. Peng-Robinson residuals are around
  $10^{-2}$ and $Z$ is order 1, and an unscaled GMM fits the wrong covariance.
- Students expect conditioning on $r = 0$ to return exactly the roots. It returns a
  distribution around them whose width is the surrogate's error; have them cluster the
  samples and then polish each cluster with a few Newton steps, which is the honest workflow.
- Conditional flow matching can only condition on what was fed to the network in training.
  A GMM can condition on any subset after the fact. This is the most common confusion when
  comparing the two.
- Students will ask whether this beats `scipy.optimize`. It does not, when a model exists;
  the paper says so. Its uses are finding every solution at once, working from data when no
  model exists, and giving a solver good starting points.
- Keep the survey to "what is generated, and how it was checked". Without that question it
  turns into a gallery of pictures.
- Do not point forward to L17 in the notes or slides. The chain-rule factorization is
  introduced on its own terms as the third family; the connection makes itself when L17 is
  released.
