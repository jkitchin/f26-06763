## Dataset: a live plant stream
A [Tennessee Eastman](https://doi.org/10.1016/0098-1354(93)80018-I) plant simulation, running continuously on a machine in the department and publishing every sample as an MQTT message. Downs and Vogel's 1993 model is the standard benchmark problem in chemical process control: a real plant, published with its dynamics and disturbances but not its true kinetics, so that control and monitoring methods could be compared on something harder than a tank of water. L5 used the [Rieth et al. archive](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/6C3JR1) of that model as files. This is the same plant, arriving one message at a time.

Connect to `kitchin-services.cheme.cmu.edu` on port 443, WebSocket path `/mqtt`, and subscribe to `plant/#`. It is anonymous and read-only: there is no account, and nobody subscribed can publish, so the feed you collect cannot be contaminated by the rest of the class.

**The plant runs 144 times faster than the wall clock**, and samples every 180 plant-seconds, which is the interval the published TEP datasets use. One message lands every 1.25 seconds, and **ten minutes of collecting is a full plant day**: 480 messages, about 25,000 readings, roughly 3 MB. Collect at least ten minutes. Longer is fine and costs you nothing but disk.

### Twenty disturbances, and they switch off again
The model defines twenty numbered disturbances, `IDV(1)` through `IDV(20)`, ranging from a step change in a feed composition to a valve that sticks. They are the `faultNumber` L5 grouped by. The plant runs one episode every twelve plant hours, so **your ten minutes contains two of them** wherever in the day you happen to start, and the schedule is not published.

The difference from the files you have seen is worth expecting, because it changes the shape of what you are looking for. Every published TEP dataset starts a disturbance and then runs to the end of the file, so a fault there is a step you never come back from. Here each episode **clears again** after one and a half to four plant hours, and the plant recovers. What you are looking for is an interval with two edges, not a change of level that persists.

They are not equally visible. Some are step changes that any method finds; some are slow random walks in a variable nobody measures directly, and a few plant hours of those are genuinely hard to separate from normal operation. That is the honest situation a monitoring system is in, and it is why task 5 grades your method rather than your answer.

## What arrives
Two topics. `plant/tep/birth` carries one retained message describing the plant, delivered the moment you subscribe. `plant/tep/telemetry` carries the samples.

The collector wraps each message in an envelope that records when *your* machine saw it:

```
{"receivedAt": "2026-09-09T15:26:27.699Z", "topic": "plant/tep/telemetry",
 "payload": { ... the broker's bytes, unaltered ... }}
```

The birth message is your data contract. It names every tag, its description and its unit, and it tells you how to convert between the two clocks:

```json
{"type": "birth", "plant": "tep", "schema": "opcua-datavalue/1",
 "accelerationFactor": 144, "plantEpoch": "2026-09-09T14:55:46.480Z",
 "sampleIntervalSeconds": 180,
 "tags": [{"name": "XMEAS_1_A_feed", "description": "A Feed (stream 1)", "unit": "kscmh"},
          {"name": "XMEAS_2_D_feed", "description": "D Feed (stream 2)", "unit": "kg/hr"}]}
```

Two of the 53 tags are shown; the real message carries all of them.

There are 53 tags: 41 measurements (`XMEAS_*`) and 12 manipulated variables (`XMV_*`). Build your schema from this message rather than typing the tag list out, and a channel added to the plant next semester will not silently fall out of your pipeline.

**L5's files had 52 columns and this has 53, and you have not double-counted.** The published archives drop `XMV_12`, the reactor agitator speed, because the standard control scheme holds it constant and a column that never moves is dead weight in a file. A live simulator has no reason to drop it, so here it is. Expect it to sit still.

**Read the descriptions and units before you start.** Every tag arrives with both, and they are the difference between reporting that columns 11, 12 and 13 moved and reporting that the separator moved. The names follow the plant in flow order: the feeds and recycle (`XMEAS_1` to `XMEAS_6`), the reactor (`7` to `9`), the purge (`10`), the separator (`11` to `14`), the stripper (`15` to `19`), then compressor work and the two cooling-water outlets (`20` to `22`), and finally the compositions (`23` to `41`). The twelve `XMV_*` are the valves and setpoints a controller moves, ending with the agitator above.

A telemetry message is [Sparkplug B](https://sparkplug.eclipse.org/specification/) shaped on the outside and [OPC UA](https://reference.opcfoundation.org/Core/Part4/v105/docs/7.11) inside:

```json
{"seq": 197, "timestamp": "2026-09-12T16:37:45.479Z", "isHistorical": false,
 "metrics": [
   {"name": "XMEAS_1_A_feed",  "value": 0.253256,  "statusCode": "Good",
    "sourceTimestamp": "2026-09-12T16:37:45.479Z"},
   {"name": "XMEAS_23_feed_A", "value": 31.551008, "statusCode": "Good",
    "sourceTimestamp": "2026-09-12T16:34:45.479Z"}]}
```

Read those two metrics again. They arrived in the same message and they are **three plant minutes apart**, and that is not a bug.

### Three clocks, and none of them is interchangeable

**`sourceTimestamp` is event time.** It belongs to the measurement, it is stamped by the instrument, and it never changes no matter how many systems forward the record. Every window you compute should be defined on this.

**`timestamp` on the message is the OPC UA `ServerTimestamp`**, the moment the batch was assembled. It is carried once instead of fifty-three times because a batch is forwarded as a unit. It runs on the plant clock, it is monotonic, and it is not when the measurement happened.

**`receivedAt` is processing time**, stamped by your laptop off your own wall clock when the message landed. It is the only wall-clock stamp anywhere in the pipeline.

The first two are on the plant clock, which starts at `plantEpoch` and runs 144× fast. The third is on yours. Subtracting one from the other produces a number, and the number is meaningless. After an hour of collecting, the plant clock is six days past where it started.

### Nineteen tags report the past

`XMEAS_23` through `XMEAS_41` are composition analysers rather than instruments. They sample on a schedule and each reports the composition of its *previous* sample, so the value is stale by up to a full analyser interval and holds still in between while the other thirty-four channels move every sample. Fourteen gas channels alternate between a lag of 0 and 180 s; five product channels cycle through 0, 180, 360, 540 and 720 s. **A student who assumes one timestamp per message gets those nineteen channels wrong by up to twelve minutes**, and all nineteen are compositions, which is exactly where a fault-detection model looks.

### The link is lossy, and it fails the way links fail

The connection does not drop a random 2% of messages. It goes down, the publisher buffers into a bounded queue, and when it comes back the backlog drains alongside the live stream. Late data therefore arrives **in bursts**, and the worst case your watermark has to survive is the length of an outage rather than an average delay. Measured over 6,000 messages at these settings:

| | |
|---|---|
| records arriving after a newer one | 13.6% |
| lateness, plant minutes | median 51, p95 117, max 198 |
| samples never delivered at all | 20 in 5,961 |
| duplicate deliveries | 59 |
| `Uncertain` / `Bad` status codes | 648 / 146 in 318,000 |

Your ten minutes will not match these exactly, and are not expected to. `isHistorical` is honest: every late record carries it and every record carrying it is late. That does not make the assignment easy, because knowing a record is late tells you nothing about what to do with it.

## Tips on the parts that are new

### The broker is your log, and your file is your commit log
L6 introduced the log abstraction with Kafka. You are not running Kafka, and you do not need to: MQTT gives you the same three properties the concept rests on. Messages carry an increasing sequence number, delivery is at-least-once, and a subscriber reads a durable ordered stream. `a03-collect.py` writes each message to a file as it lands, in arrival order, unmodified. That file is your commit log, and every stage after it replays from the file rather than from the network. A stream is gone once it has gone past; the first thing to do with one is write it down.

### `seq` wraps at 256, and it is not a primary key
Sparkplug's `seq` is a single byte that rolls over. In a ten-minute collection it rolls over. Deduplicating on it will report hundreds of duplicates that are not duplicates. **Deduplicate at the record level on `(tag, sourceTimestamp)`**, which is the pair that identifies a measurement.

That key has a consequence worth expecting: a latched analyser reports the same reading in several consecutive messages, with the same `sourceTimestamp` each time, so deduplication collapses them. Your tidy table will hold fewer than 53 rows per sample, and that is correct rather than lost data. One measurement, reported repeatedly, is one measurement.

### Dedup makes the pipeline idempotent, which you should test
At-least-once means running your pipeline twice over the same file, or over a file you collected more into, must not double-count anything. Run it twice and compare the outputs. This is a property you can check in one line, and it is the practical reason exactly-once is usually built as at-least-once plus an idempotent write.

### Windows over event time
Bucket on `event_time`, not on `received_at` and not on the message `timestamp`. Pandas will do it with `df.groupby(pd.Grouper(key="event_time", freq="30min"))` or `.resample()`; Polars has `group_by_dynamic`. The last window in your collection is incomplete, because the samples that would fill it had not been generated when you disconnected. Say what you did with it.

Averaging a latched analyser channel over a window is the trap here. Its readings are not evenly spaced in event time, so a plain mean weights whichever value happened to be republished more often. Task 5 has you resample onto the regular sample grid before windowing, which fixes the weighting and raises the missing-data question at the same time.

### Long form, wide form, and the query plan
The stream arrives long, one row per reading, which is the right shape for validating and deduplicating, because every row carries its own status and its own timestamp. An analysis across tags wants the other shape, one row per window and one column per tag, because that is what a correlation, a control chart or a Hotelling statistic is defined over. `pivot` goes one way in either library and `unpivot` (`melt` in pandas) comes back.

Task 2 asks for Polars and for a `LazyFrame`. Polars does not run your expressions as you write them: it builds a plan, rewrites it, and executes when you `.collect()`. `.explain()` prints that plan, and what to look for is the difference between what you wrote and what it will run, such as projections pushed down into the scan or columns it worked out it never needed.

`pl.scan_ndjson` infers the schema from the **first 100 lines by default**, and your file holds two different payload shapes. If the birth message is not in that first hundred, which is what an appended second capture looks like, its fields are dropped from the inferred schema with no error and no warning, and you get a file that reads fine and is missing the tag dictionary. Pass `infer_schema_length=None`, or declare the schema yourself. Inferring a contract from a sample of the data is the failure that task 3 exists to prevent, and here it is one line above it.

Do not expect L5's timing comparison to reproduce at this scale. Ten minutes of collecting is about 25,000 rows, small enough that both libraries finish before either has done anything interesting, and most of what you would measure is interpreter startup. The size at which the choice starts to matter is several orders of magnitude above this.

### The 53 tags do not share a grid, and the wide table has holes in it
The continuous channels stamp the instant they were sampled. The analysers stamp the instant their last analysis finished. Pivot your deduplicated table straight to wide form and the union of those two sets of timestamps becomes the index, so every analyser column is empty at every instant that belongs to somebody else. Thirty plant hours comes out around 603 rows by 53 columns with a fifth of the cells empty, split roughly 0.2% across the continuous columns and 58% across the analyser columns, and fewer than one row in ten complete. Anything defined over complete cases throws away most of the plant day before it starts.

Two different holes are mixed together in that table and they want different answers. An analyser cell is empty because the instrument has not finished a new analysis, and the composition it is reporting is still the one from the last analysis, so carrying the value forward repeats what the instrument is saying. A continuous cell is empty because a message never arrived, and the value moved while nobody was looking, so carrying it forward invents a measurement. Both fills are defensible on a plant dashboard, and a reader who cannot tell which cells were filled has no way to check either one, so task 5 asks for the two counts and for a record of what you filled.

### pandera
[pandera](https://pandera.readthedocs.io/) lets you declare a `DataFrameSchema` and validate a dataframe against it. Two things earn it the import here, against a wall of `assert`. `lazy=True` collects every failure instead of raising on the first, so one run tells you everything that is wrong. And `SchemaErrors.failure_cases` hands you the offending rows as a dataframe, with a column, a check name and a row index each, so quarantine is two lines.

**Install it as `pandera[pandas]` and import it as `pandera.pandas`.** Version 0.30 moved the pandas classes out of the top level, and `import pandera as pa` still works today while emitting a `FutureWarning` that says it will stop. Every tutorial written before 2025 uses the old spelling.

```python
import pandera.pandas as pa

schema = pa.DataFrameSchema({
    "tag":    pa.Column(str, pa.Check.isin(tag_names)),
    "value":  pa.Column(float, nullable=False),
    "status": pa.Column(str),
})
try:
    schema.validate(df, lazy=True)
except pa.errors.SchemaErrors as e:
    bad = e.failure_cases        # column, check, failure_case, index
```

Build the schema *from the birth message*: iterate its `tags` list to generate a per-tag range check, so the contract the plant published is the contract you enforce.

### Quarantine, not `dropna`
`Bad_DeviceFailure` and `Uncertain_SensorNotAccurate` records are the instrument telling you it does not trust itself. Deleting them destroys the evidence and makes the count unrecoverable; keeping them silently poisons every average. Write them to a separate table with their reason, count them, and exclude them from the analysis. A pipeline that cannot say how much it threw away cannot be audited.

**5. Fill the grid, then window and find the plant's bad day.** Three parts.

First reindex onto a regular grid. Build `processed/grid.parquet` from your deduplicated, quarantined table, with one row per 180-plant-second sample instant between the first and the last in your collection, and one column per tag. A per-window score against the quiet stretch is enough, and a control chart or a simple multivariate statistic is more than enough. Report when it started, when it ended, and which tags moved.

You are graded on the method and on your honesty about its limits, **not on naming the fault**. Some of these disturbances are slow random walks that are genuinely hard to see in a few plant hours, and a report that says "these three windows are anomalous, these two tags moved, and I cannot tell this from noise" is worth more than a confident wrong answer.
